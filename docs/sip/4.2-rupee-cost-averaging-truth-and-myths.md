# Rupee Cost Averaging, Truth and Myths

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

## In plain words

Think of buying vegetables at a local market every week, instead of buying a year's supply
in one visit. Some weeks prices are low, some weeks high, but buying regularly means you
end up paying something close to the average price over the year, rather than betting
everything on one uncertain day's price. **Rupee cost averaging** applies this same idea
to buying mutual fund units through a SIP.

## How it works

### What rupee cost averaging means

**Rupee cost averaging** - the effect of investing a fixed amount at regular intervals,
which results in buying more units when NAV (introduced in Unit 3.1) is lower, and fewer
units when NAV is higher, so the average cost per unit can end up lower than the simple
average of the NAVs over that period.

### A worked example

Consider a SIP of ₹2,000 per month for four months, with NAV moving as shown.

| Month | SIP amount | NAV | Units allotted |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹2,000 | ₹20 | 100.0 |
| 2 | ₹2,000 | ₹10 | 200.0 |
| 3 | ₹2,000 | ₹16 | 125.0 |
| 4 | ₹2,000 | ₹20 | 100.0 |
| Total | ₹8,000 | - | 525.0 |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast.

```
average cost per unit = total amount invested / total units allotted
                      = ₹8,000 / 525.0 units
                      ≈ ₹15.24 per unit
```

The simple average of the four NAVs (₹20, ₹10, ₹16, ₹20) is ₹16.50. The rupee-cost-averaged
cost of about ₹15.24 is lower than this simple average, because more units were bought in
Month 2, when NAV was lowest.

### When rupee cost averaging genuinely helps

1. It helps most when NAV moves up and down significantly over the investment period,
   rather than moving steadily in one direction.
2. It helps investors avoid needing to correctly time when NAV is at its lowest, since
   installments happen automatically regardless of market conditions.
3. It builds an investing habit, which supports staying invested through volatility, as
   discussed further as investor behaviour in Unit 7.4.

### When it does not help, or barely matters

| Situation | Effect on rupee cost averaging |
|---|---|
| NAV rises steadily, with little movement down | Averaging benefit is small; a lumpsum invested at the start would likely have done better |
| NAV falls steadily over a long period, without recovery | Averaging softens losses somewhat, but does not prevent losses if the trend does not reverse |
| A very short SIP period (a few installments only) | Limited number of data points, so the averaging effect is weaker |

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Believing rupee cost averaging guarantees a profit; it only affects the average cost per
  unit, not whether the investment ultimately gains or loses value.
- Assuming rupee cost averaging always beats a lumpsum investment; in steadily rising
  markets, lumpsum can outperform.
- Stopping a SIP during a market fall, which removes the exact months where more units are
  being bought at a lower cost.
- Treating a SIP as a mechanism to "beat the market", rather than a disciplined way to
  invest regularly.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Rupee cost averaging is a mathematical effect of investing at intervals, not a promise or
guarantee of profit. If the underlying scheme's investments lose value over the relevant
period and do not recover, a SIP can still result in an overall loss.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Academic finance literature and NISM workbooks note that rupee cost averaging, sometimes
called dollar cost averaging internationally, reduces the risk of investing a large amount
at a single unfavourable time, but does not increase expected long-term returns compared to
a lumpsum investment in a market with a steady upward long-term trend. Its main practical
benefit, referenced in AMFI investor education material, is behavioural: it removes the
need to time individual purchases and supports consistent, disciplined investing over long
periods, connecting directly to the investor behaviour concepts in Unit 7.4.
:::

## Quick recap

- Rupee cost averaging results in buying more units when NAV is low and fewer when NAV is
  high.
- It genuinely helps most when NAV fluctuates significantly, rather than moving steadily
  in one direction.
- It does not guarantee profit, and can underperform a lumpsum investment in a steadily
  rising market.
- Its main real benefit is behavioural: removing the need to time the market and
  supporting disciplined, regular investing.

## Check yourself

1. In the worked example, why was the average cost per unit lower than the simple average
   of the NAVs?
   ::: details Show answer
   Because more units were bought in Month 2, when NAV was at its lowest, pulling the
   average cost per unit below the simple average of all four NAVs.
   :::
2. Does rupee cost averaging guarantee that an investment will be profitable?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. It only affects the average cost per unit bought. If the underlying investment loses
   value overall, a SIP can still result in a loss.
   :::
3. In which type of market movement does rupee cost averaging help the most?
   ::: details Show answer
   It helps most when NAV moves up and down significantly, rather than moving steadily in
   one direction.
   :::
4. Why might stopping a SIP during a market fall work against the benefit of rupee cost
   averaging?
   ::: details Show answer
   A market fall is exactly when more units are bought at a lower cost. Stopping the SIP
   during this period removes those lower-cost purchases from the averaging effect.
   :::
