# What a SIP Is

## In plain words

Think of a school fee paid in monthly installments instead of one large yearly payment.
Each installment is smaller and easier to manage, and you do not need to arrange a large
sum all at once. A **SIP** works the same way for investing.

## How it works

### What a SIP is

**SIP** (Systematic Investment Plan) - a facility that lets an investor invest a fixed
amount in a mutual fund scheme at regular intervals, usually monthly, instead of investing
a large amount at once.

1. You set up a SIP for a chosen scheme, amount, and date (for example, ₹5,000 on the 5th
   of every month).
2. On each SIP date, the fixed amount is debited from your bank account, following the
   money trail described in Unit 2.2.
3. Units are allotted at that specific day's NAV (introduced in Unit 3.1), not at a fixed
   or average price decided in advance.
4. Over time, this results in units bought at many different NAVs, across market ups and
   downs.

### SIP vs lumpsum

**Lumpsum investment** - investing a single, larger amount at one point in time, rather
than spreading it across installments.

| Feature | SIP | Lumpsum |
|---|---|---|
| Amount per transaction | Smaller, fixed, regular | Larger, one-time |
| Timing risk | Spread across many dates and NAVs | Concentrated at one date and NAV |
| Suits | Regular income, disciplined ongoing investing | A large amount available at once |
| Market timing needed | Less critical, due to spreading over time | More critical, since entire amount enters at once |

Neither approach is universally "better"; the right choice depends on how the money
becomes available (regular income vs a lump sum received at once) and personal comfort
with timing decisions.

### STP and SWP, briefly

**STP** (Systematic Transfer Plan) - a facility to automatically transfer a fixed amount,
at regular intervals, from one mutual fund scheme to another, often used to move money
gradually from a debt fund into an equity fund, or vice versa.

**SWP** (Systematic Withdrawal Plan) - a facility to automatically redeem a fixed amount,
at regular intervals, from a mutual fund scheme, often used to generate a regular cash
flow from existing investments, such as during retirement.

| Facility | What moves | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| SIP | Money from your bank | Into a mutual fund scheme |
| STP | Money within mutual funds | Between two mutual fund schemes |
| SWP | Money from a mutual fund scheme | Out to your bank account |

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Aarav sets up a SIP of ₹3,000 per month in an equity fund.

| Month | SIP amount | NAV on SIP date (illustrative) | Units allotted (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | ₹3,000 | ₹30 | 100 |
| Month 2 | ₹3,000 | ₹25 | 120 |
| Month 3 | ₹3,000 | ₹33 | 90.9 |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. Aarav's ₹3,000 buys a different number of
units each month, because NAV changes with market movements. This pattern of buying more
units when NAV is lower and fewer units when NAV is higher is explored fully in Unit 4.2.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Assuming a SIP guarantees profit, or automatically protects against market risk
  (introduced in Unit 1.3).
- Confusing SIP with a recurring bank deposit; a SIP's returns depend on the underlying
  scheme's market performance, not a fixed rate.
- Setting up an STP or SWP without understanding it as separate from a simple SIP.
- Not checking whether SIP installments are actually being processed each month, since
  failed installments are covered in Unit 4.3.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
A SIP is a way of investing regularly; it is not a separate, guaranteed-return product. The
underlying mutual fund scheme's risk, described by its riskometer in Unit 3.5, still
applies to money invested through a SIP.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
NISM and AMFI investor education material frame a SIP as an investment discipline tool
rather than a distinct asset class or product. From a portfolio theory perspective,
referenced in Prasanna Chandra's *Investment Analysis and Portfolio Management*, spreading
purchases over time using a SIP is one practical way retail investors attempt to manage
timing risk without needing to actively predict market movements, a concept explored
further as rupee cost averaging in Unit 4.2.
:::

## Quick recap

- A SIP invests a fixed amount at regular intervals, buying units at each date's NAV.
- Lumpsum investing puts a larger amount in at one point in time, concentrating timing
  risk.
- STP moves money between two mutual fund schemes; SWP moves money out of a scheme to your
  bank account.
- A SIP does not guarantee profit or remove the underlying scheme's market risk.

## Check yourself

1. At what price are units allotted for each SIP installment?
   ::: details Show answer
   At that specific day's NAV, which changes with market movements, not at a fixed or
   pre-decided price.
   :::
2. What is the main difference between a SIP and a lumpsum investment?
   ::: details Show answer
   A SIP spreads a fixed amount across many regular installments, spreading timing risk over
   time. A lumpsum invests a larger amount at one point in time, concentrating timing risk
   at that single date.
   :::
3. What does an STP do?
   ::: details Show answer
   It automatically transfers a fixed amount, at regular intervals, from one mutual fund
   scheme to another.
   :::
4. Does setting up a SIP remove the underlying mutual fund scheme's market risk?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. A SIP is a method of investing regularly; it does not remove or guarantee protection
   from the underlying scheme's market risk.
   :::
