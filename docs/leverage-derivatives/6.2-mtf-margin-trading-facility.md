# MTF (Margin Trading Facility)

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What MTF is and how it lets you buy shares with borrowed money
- How interest is charged on the borrowed portion
- What pledging shares means in this context
- Worked examples of both a gain and a loss using MTF
:::

## In plain words

Think of buying a two-wheeler partly with your own savings and partly with a loan from
the dealer, using the vehicle itself as security for that loan. **MTF** lets you buy
shares in a similar way: paying part of the cost yourself, and borrowing the rest from
your broker, with the shares themselves used as security.

## How it works

### What MTF is

**MTF** (Margin Trading Facility) - a facility offered by SEBI-registered brokers,
letting an investor buy shares (introduced in Unit 5.1) by paying only a portion of the
total value upfront, as margin (introduced in Unit 6.1), with the broker funding the
remaining amount.

### Interest on the borrowed amount

The broker charges **interest** on the borrowed portion of an MTF position, for as long as
the position remains open, in addition to normal brokerage and other charges (covered in
full in Unit 8.1).

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
MTF interest rates, margin requirements, and eligible stocks vary by broker and are
subject to SEBI rules that can change. Confirm the current MTF interest rate and terms on
your specific broker's website before using this facility.
:::

### Pledging shares

**Pledging** - the process of marking shares as collateral in favour of the broker, as
part of using MTF or similar margin facilities, so that the broker has a formal claim over
those shares as security for the amount lent. Shares bought under MTF, or shares used to
provide additional margin, are typically pledged in this way.

### Worked example: a gain

Anil buys shares worth ₹1,00,000 under MTF, paying ₹40,000 of his own money and borrowing
₹60,000 from the broker, at an illustrative MTF interest rate of 12% per year.

| Item | Value (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Total position value | ₹1,00,000 |
| Anil's own margin | ₹40,000 |
| Amount borrowed | ₹60,000 |
| Share price rises by 10% after 1 month | New position value: ₹1,10,000 |
| Approximate interest cost for 1 month (12% per year on ₹60,000) | ₹600 |
| Gross gain on position | ₹10,000 |
| Net gain after interest | ₹9,400 |
| Return on Anil's own ₹40,000 | Approximately 23.5% for the month |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. The 10% rise in the share price becomes a
much larger percentage return on Anil's own money, due to leverage, echoing the
magnification effect introduced in Unit 6.1.

### Worked example: a loss

Using the same setup, if the share price instead falls by 10% after 1 month:

| Item | Value (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Total position value | ₹1,00,000 |
| Anil's own margin | ₹40,000 |
| Amount borrowed | ₹60,000 |
| Share price falls by 10% after 1 month | New position value: ₹90,000 |
| Approximate interest cost for 1 month | ₹600 |
| Gross loss on position | ₹10,000 |
| Net loss after interest | ₹10,600 |
| Loss on Anil's own ₹40,000 | Approximately 26.5% for the month |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. A 10% fall in the share price becomes a
substantially larger percentage loss on Anil's own money, and interest continues to accrue
regardless of whether the position gains or loses value.

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Ignoring the ongoing interest cost when estimating potential MTF returns.
- Forgetting that a loss under MTF can exceed the percentage loss on the underlying share
  price, due to leverage.
- Not tracking margin requirements closely, risking a margin call or auto square-off,
  covered in Unit 6.1.
- Assuming all shares are eligible for MTF; brokers and exchanges specify which stocks
  qualify.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
MTF combines leverage risk with an ongoing interest cost that applies regardless of
whether the position gains or loses value. Losses can accumulate faster than with a
regular, non-leveraged share purchase. This facility is not suitable for beginners without
a clear understanding of margin calls and auto square-off, covered in Unit 6.1.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
SEBI's framework for Margin Trading Facility specifies eligible securities, maximum
funding limits, and margin requirements that brokers must follow, referenced in NISM
derivatives and margin trading workbooks. Because MTF funding is essentially a
broker-provided loan secured against pledged shares, its risk profile combines both
market risk on the underlying shares and the fixed obligation to service interest,
regardless of the position's performance, similar in principle to using a loan to buy any
other asset.
:::

## Quick recap

- MTF lets you buy shares by paying only part of the value upfront, with the broker
  funding the rest.
- Interest is charged on the borrowed portion for as long as the position stays open.
- Pledging marks shares as collateral in favour of the broker.
- MTF magnifies both gains and losses on your own invested money, and interest accrues
  regardless of the position's performance.

## Check yourself

1. What does pledging shares mean in the context of MTF?
   ::: details Show answer
   It means marking those shares as collateral in favour of the broker, giving the broker a
   formal claim over them as security for the amount lent.
   :::
2. Does MTF interest apply only when the position is profitable?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. Interest on the borrowed portion accrues for as long as the position remains open,
   regardless of whether the position is gaining or losing value.
   :::
3. In the worked loss example, why was the percentage loss on Anil's own money larger than
   the 10% fall in the share price?
   ::: details Show answer
   Because leverage magnifies percentage losses on the investor's own money, and the ongoing
   interest cost added further to the loss.
   :::
4. Is MTF generally considered suitable for beginners?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. It combines leverage risk with an ongoing interest cost, and requires a clear
   understanding of margin calls and auto square-off before use.
   :::
