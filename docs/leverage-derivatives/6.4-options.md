# Options

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What a call option and a put option are
- What premium, strike price and expiry mean for options
- What time value is

:::

## In plain words

Think of paying a small, non-refundable booking fee to reserve the right, but not the
obligation, to buy a flat at a fixed price within the next three months. If the flat's
market value rises well above that fixed price, you can still buy it cheap. If it falls,
you can simply walk away, losing only your booking fee. An **option** works on this same
principle of a right, without an obligation, in exchange for a fee.

## How it works

### Call and put options

A **call option** - a contract giving the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to buy
an underlying asset at a specified price, within a specified period. A **put option** - a
contract giving the buyer the right, but not the obligation, to sell an underlying asset
at a specified price, within a specified period.

| Option type | Right given to the buyer |
|---|---|
| Call option | Right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price |
| Put option | Right to sell the underlying asset at the strike price |

### Premium

**Premium** - the price paid by the option buyer to the option seller, to acquire this
right. The premium is paid upfront and is generally non-refundable, regardless of whether
the option is later exercised.

### Strike price

**Strike price** - the fixed price at which the option buyer can buy (for a call) or sell
(for a put) the underlying asset, if they choose to exercise the option.

### Expiry, revisited

**Expiry**, introduced for futures in Unit 6.3, also applies to options: the date by which
the option must be exercised or it lapses, becoming worthless if not in the buyer's
favour.

### Time value

An option's premium generally consists of two parts: **intrinsic value** (the immediate
benefit if exercised right now, if any) and **time value** - the additional amount buyers
are willing to pay for the possibility that the option becomes more valuable before
expiry. Time value generally declines as expiry approaches, a pattern sometimes called
time decay.

### Buyer vs seller risk

| Role | Maximum possible loss | Maximum possible gain |
|---|---|---|
| Option buyer | Limited to the premium paid | Can be substantial, depending on how far the price moves in their favour |
| Option seller (writer) | Can be substantial, depending on how far the price moves against them | Limited to the premium received |

This asymmetry is a critical concept: an option buyer's loss is capped at the premium
paid, but an option seller's potential loss is generally much larger and, for some
positions, theoretically unlimited.

### Worked example: buying a call option

Suppose a stock trades at ₹100. An investor buys a call option with a strike price of
₹105, paying a premium of ₹3 per share, with a lot size of 500 shares (illustrative).

| Scenario at expiry | Stock price | Outcome for the buyer (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Price stays at ₹100 | ₹100 | Option lapses worthless; buyer loses the full premium: ₹3 x 500 = ₹1,500 |
| Price rises to ₹108 | ₹108 | Buyer's gain: (₹108 - ₹105 - ₹3) x 500 = ₹0 (breakeven at ₹108) |
| Price rises to ₹115 | ₹115 | Buyer's gain: (₹115 - ₹105 - ₹3) x 500 = ₹3,500 |
| Price falls to ₹90 | ₹90 | Option lapses worthless; buyer loses the full premium: ₹1,500 |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. Notice the buyer's maximum loss across every
scenario is capped at the ₹1,500 premium paid, regardless of how far the price falls.

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Believing option buying always carries lower risk than option selling; while the
  buyer's loss is capped, that capped amount, the premium, can still be a total loss.
- Selling (writing) options without understanding the potentially large, sometimes
  unlimited, loss exposure involved.
- Ignoring time decay, and holding options too close to expiry expecting a large price
  move that does not happen in time.
- Confusing strike price with the current market price of the underlying asset.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Selling (writing) options, in particular, carries a risk of loss that can significantly
exceed the premium received, and in some structures can be very large relative to the
margin deposited. This is one of the higher-risk activities covered in this book, and is
not suitable for beginners without a thorough understanding of margin requirements and
worst-case scenarios.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Hull's *Options, Futures and Other Derivatives* provides the formal framework for option
pricing, including models like Black-Scholes, which estimate an option's fair premium
based on factors including the underlying price, strike price, time to expiry, volatility
(introduced in Unit 1.3), and interest rates. The asymmetric risk profile between buyers
and sellers, described above, is a foundational concept in derivatives theory, and is a
primary reason SEBI's regulatory framework imposes stricter margin and eligibility
requirements on option sellers than on option buyers.
:::

## Quick recap

- A call option gives the right to buy, and a put option gives the right to sell, the
  underlying asset at a fixed strike price.
- Premium is the upfront, generally non-refundable price paid for this right.
- An option's value has an intrinsic value component and a time value component, which
  declines as expiry approaches.
- An option buyer's maximum loss is capped at the premium paid; an option seller's
  potential loss can be substantially larger.

## Check yourself

1. What is the key difference between a call option and a put option?
   ::: details Show answer
   A call option gives the buyer the right to buy the underlying asset at the strike price.
   A put option gives the buyer the right to sell the underlying asset at the strike price.
   :::
2. What is the maximum possible loss for an option buyer?
   ::: details Show answer
   The premium paid, since the buyer can simply let the option lapse if it is not in their
   favour, rather than being obligated to exercise it.
   :::
3. Why is time value generally higher for an option with more time left until expiry?
   ::: details Show answer
   More time until expiry means more opportunity for the underlying price to move
   favourably before the option lapses, so buyers are generally willing to pay more for that
   possibility.
   :::
4. Why is selling (writing) options generally considered riskier than buying options?
   ::: details Show answer
   An option seller's potential loss can be substantially larger than the premium received,
   and in some structures can be very large, unlike a buyer's loss, which is capped at the
   premium paid.
   :::
