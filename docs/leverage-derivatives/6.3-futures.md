## In plain words

Think of a wholesale vegetable trader agreeing today with a farmer on a fixed price for a
harvest to be delivered in three months, regardless of what the market price turns out to
be then. This is the basic idea behind a **futures contract**: an agreement made today, to
buy or sell something at a fixed price, on a specified future date.

## How it works

### What a futures contract is

A **futures contract** - a standardised agreement, traded on an exchange, to buy or sell a
specified quantity of an underlying asset (such as a stock or an index) at a predetermined
price, on a specified future date.

Futures are a type of **derivative** - a financial instrument whose value is derived from
the price of an underlying asset, rather than having independent value of its own.

### Lot size

**Lot size** - the fixed quantity of the underlying asset represented by one futures
contract, standardised by the exchange for each specific contract. You cannot buy or sell
futures in quantities smaller than one lot.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
Lot sizes for stock and index futures are set by exchanges and revised periodically,
including recent changes aimed at increasing minimum contract values. [VERIFY: check the
current minimum contract value and lot size rules on the NSE or SEBI website, as these
have been revised in recent years.]
:::

### Expiry

**Expiry** - the specified date on which a futures contract must be settled, after which
the contract ceases to exist. Indian stock and index futures commonly expire on a monthly
basis, on a specified day of the expiry month, though this can vary by exchange rules.

### Mark-to-market settlement

**Mark-to-market (MTM)** - the daily process of settling gains and losses on an open
futures position, based on that day's closing price, rather than waiting until the
contract's final expiry to settle the entire gain or loss at once.

1. At the end of each trading day, the exchange calculates the day's gain or loss on your
   open futures position, based on the day's closing price versus the previous
   reference price.
2. If your position gained value that day, the gain is credited to your account.
3. If your position lost value that day, the loss is debited from your account, and may
   require you to add margin if your balance falls below the required minimum, as
   described in Unit 6.1.
4. This process repeats every trading day until the position is closed or reaches expiry.

### Hedging vs speculation

**Hedging** - using a futures (or other derivative) position to reduce or offset an
existing risk, such as a stock holding already owned. **Speculation** - taking a futures
position specifically to profit from an expected price movement, without necessarily
holding an offsetting position in the underlying asset.

| Purpose | Example |
|---|---|
| Hedging | An investor holding shares sells futures on the same shares, to reduce the impact of a possible price fall |
| Speculation | A trader buys index futures, expecting the index to rise, without holding the underlying index components |

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Consider a simplified index futures position, illustrative and not a forecast.

| Day | Closing price of futures | Daily mark-to-market gain or loss |
|---|---|---|
| Day 1 (position opened) | 20,000 | - |
| Day 2 | 20,150 | +150 points credited |
| Day 3 | 19,900 | -250 points debited |
| Day 4 | 20,050 | +150 points credited |

If each point is worth ₹1 per unit of lot size, illustrative and not a forecast, these
daily point movements translate into daily cash gains or losses credited or debited to the
trader's account, rather than one single settlement at expiry.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Confusing a futures position with simply owning the underlying shares or index directly.
- Not accounting for daily mark-to-market debits, which can require adding funds even
  before expiry.
- Using futures for speculation while believing it is a form of hedging, without an actual
  offsetting position.
- Ignoring lot size, and misjudging the actual rupee exposure of a single futures contract.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Futures carry high leverage, as introduced in Unit 6.1, and losses can be large and rapid,
including through daily mark-to-market debits. Speculative futures trading is
considerably riskier than simple delivery-based investing, and SEBI data on retail trader
outcomes, covered in Unit 6.5, shows most individual traders lose money in derivatives
over time.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Hull's *Options, Futures and Other Derivatives* describes futures as exchange-traded,
standardised versions of forward contracts, with the clearing corporation (introduced in
Unit 2.1) acting as the counterparty to every trade, and daily mark-to-market settlement
specifically designed to reduce the build-up of counterparty credit risk over the life of
the contract. This daily settlement is a key structural difference from an over-the-counter
forward contract, which typically settles only once, at maturity.
:::

## Quick recap

- A futures contract is a standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell an asset
  at a fixed price on a future date.
- Lot size fixes the minimum tradeable quantity per contract; expiry is the date the
  contract must be settled.
- Mark-to-market settles gains and losses daily, based on each day's closing price.
- Hedging uses futures to offset existing risk; speculation uses futures to bet on price
  movement without an offsetting position.

## Check yourself

1. What is a futures contract?
   ::: details Show answer
   A standardised, exchange-traded agreement to buy or sell a specified quantity of an
   underlying asset at a predetermined price, on a specified future date.
   :::
2. How does mark-to-market settlement differ from settling a contract only at expiry?
   ::: details Show answer
   Mark-to-market settles gains and losses daily, based on each day's closing price, rather
   than waiting to settle the entire gain or loss only once, at expiry.
   :::
3. What is the key difference between hedging and speculation using futures?
   ::: details Show answer
   Hedging uses a futures position to reduce or offset an existing risk, such as an already
   owned holding. Speculation takes a futures position to profit from an expected price
   move, without an offsetting position.
   :::
4. Why can daily mark-to-market debits create an unexpected need for additional funds?
   ::: details Show answer
   A losing day's mark-to-market debit reduces your account balance, and if it falls below
   the required margin, you may need to add funds to avoid a margin call or auto
   square-off.
   :::
