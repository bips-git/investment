# Goals and Time Horizon

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What goal-based investing means
- Why an emergency fund should generally come before other investing
- Why term and health insurance are usually a prerequisite, not an afterthought
- How to think about time horizon for each goal
:::

## In plain words

Think of packing for several different trips at once: a day trip, a week-long family
visit, and a year-long relocation. Each trip needs a different bag, packed differently,
based on how long you will be away and what you will need. Investing works the same way:
different goals, with different time horizons, call for different approaches, rather than
one single plan for all your money.

## How it works

### Goal-based investing

**Goal-based investing** - planning and managing investments around specific financial
goals, each with its own amount needed, time horizon, and priority, rather than investing
without a defined purpose.

1. List each financial goal: for example, an emergency fund, a family trip, a child's
   education, a home down payment, or retirement.
2. Estimate the approximate amount needed and the number of years until each goal.
3. Match each goal to an approach suited to its time horizon, building on the ideas in
   Unit 1.4 and Unit 3.2.
4. Review and adjust goals periodically, as income, priorities, or circumstances change.

### Emergency fund first

An **emergency fund** - money set aside specifically to cover unexpected expenses or a
loss of income, kept in safe, highly liquid options such as a bank account or a liquid
fund, introduced in Unit 3.2.

A commonly cited guideline is to hold an emergency fund covering three to six months of
essential expenses, though the appropriate amount can vary based on job stability, family
responsibilities, and other personal circumstances.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
The exact recommended number of months for an emergency fund is a general guideline, not
a fixed rule, and can reasonably vary by individual circumstances. There is no single
official government-mandated figure to "check"; use your own judgement based on income
stability and responsibilities.
:::

### Term and health insurance before investing

**Term insurance** - a pure life insurance policy that pays a fixed sum to nominees if the
policyholder passes away during the policy term, without any investment or savings
component, generally offering high coverage at a relatively low premium.

**Health insurance** - a policy that covers, wholly or partly, medical treatment costs,
reducing the risk that a medical emergency forces a family to sell investments or take on
debt to cover costs.

Both are generally recommended as a foundation to arrange before significant investing,
because a serious, unexpected event without adequate insurance can force premature
withdrawal from investments, undermining the compounding and long-term growth discussed
in Unit 1.2.

| Priority order (general guideline) | Purpose |
|---|---|
| 1. Emergency fund | Covers short-term unexpected expenses or income loss |
| 2. Term insurance | Protects dependents financially if the policyholder passes away |
| 3. Health insurance | Reduces the financial impact of medical emergencies |
| 4. Goal-based investing | Builds wealth toward specific, planned goals |

This order is a general guideline, illustrative and not a universal rule; individual
circumstances, existing employer-provided cover, and family responsibilities can affect
the right sequence and amounts for a specific person.

### Time horizon and typical approach

| Time horizon | Example goal | Typical approach (see Unit 1.4, Unit 3.2) |
|---|---|---|
| Under 3 years | Emergency fund, a planned large purchase soon | Safer, highly liquid options, such as a liquid fund or bank FD |
| 3 to 7 years | A car, a wedding, a shorter-term goal | A mix of debt and moderate equity exposure |
| 7 years or more | Retirement, a child's higher education | Greater equity exposure, given more time to manage volatility (Unit 1.3) |

This table shows general, typical patterns, illustrative and not a forecast or a
recommendation for any specific product.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Jyoti lists her financial goals before deciding how to invest.

| Goal | Time horizon | Priority level |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency fund (6 months expenses) | Immediate | Highest, arranged first |
| Term and health insurance | Immediate | Arranged alongside the emergency fund |
| Child's education | 12 years | Long-term goal-based investing |
| Retirement | 28 years | Long-term goal-based investing |

This is illustrative, not a forecast. Jyoti arranges her safety net first, then begins
goal-based investing for her longer-term goals, following the general priority order
above.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Starting aggressive investing before building an adequate emergency fund.
- Treating insurance-linked investment products as a substitute for pure term insurance,
  without comparing coverage and cost carefully.
- Ignoring health insurance because of employer-provided cover, without checking if it is
  adequate or continues after a job change.
- Investing for a short-term goal in equity, exposing it to volatility risk it cannot
  absorb in time.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Without an emergency fund and adequate insurance, an unexpected event can force you to
redeem investments at a bad time, potentially at a loss, or take on high-cost debt. This
safety net is not optional; it is the foundation that protects your longer-term investing
plan.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Financial planning frameworks, referenced in NISM investment adviser workbooks, describe
this sequencing, risk protection first, then goal-based investing, as core to a sound
personal financial plan. This mirrors the risk capacity concept introduced in Unit 1.3:
adequate insurance and an emergency fund increase an individual's actual capacity to take
investment risk elsewhere, since a single adverse event is less likely to force a poorly
timed liquidation of long-term investments.
:::

## Quick recap

- Goal-based investing matches each financial goal to its own time horizon and approach.
- An emergency fund, kept safe and liquid, is generally recommended before other
  investing.
- Term and health insurance are generally a foundation to arrange before significant
  investing, not an afterthought.
- Time horizon should guide the mix of safety and growth used for each specific goal.

## Check yourself

1. Why is goal-based investing generally more useful than investing without a defined
   purpose?
   ::: details Show answer
   It matches the amount, time horizon and approach to each specific goal, rather than
   applying one single, undifferentiated plan to all money invested.
   :::
2. Why is an emergency fund generally recommended before other investing?
   ::: details Show answer
   It provides a safe, liquid buffer for unexpected expenses or income loss, reducing the
   risk of having to redeem other investments at a bad time.
   :::
3. What is the main purpose of term insurance?
   ::: details Show answer
   To provide a fixed sum to nominees if the policyholder passes away during the policy
   term, protecting dependents financially.
   :::
4. Why might a short-term goal, like a purchase planned for next year, be unsuitable for
   equity investing?
   ::: details Show answer
   Equity carries volatility, introduced in Unit 1.3, and a short time horizon leaves little
   time to recover from a downturn before the money is needed.
   :::
