# Diversification and Rebalancing

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn

- What over-diversification is

- The main methods used to rebalance a portfolio

## In plain words

Think of a school cricket team picking players from only one batting position, versus a
team with a genuine mix of batters, bowlers and all-rounders. A well-diversified team
performs more consistently across different match situations. **Diversification** applies
this same idea to investments: spreading money across different holdings so that no
single one dominates the outcome.

## How it works

### Diversification, revisited

**Diversification** - spreading investments across different securities, sectors, or
asset classes, so that poor performance in any single holding has a limited impact on the
overall portfolio. This concept was introduced briefly alongside systematic and
unsystematic risk in Unit 1.3.

### Overlap between funds

**Overlap** - when two or more mutual fund schemes an investor holds actually own many of
the same underlying stocks, reducing the real diversification benefit of holding multiple
funds, even though it looks like separate investments on paper.

| Situation | Appears diversified? | Actually diversified? |
|---|---|---|
| Holding 5 different large-cap funds from different AMCs | Yes, on paper | Often not much, since large-cap funds frequently hold many of the same top companies |
| Holding one large-cap fund, one debt fund, and one gold-linked instrument | Yes, on paper | Generally yes, since these asset classes behave quite differently |

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Assuming that holding many mutual funds automatically means good diversification,
  without checking for overlap in underlying holdings.
- Choosing multiple funds from the same category (see Unit 3.2) purely because they have
  different names or AMCs.
- Ignoring overlap when a factsheet's portfolio section (Unit 3.5) actually reveals
  similar top holdings across funds.
:::

### Over-diversification

**Over-diversification** - holding so many different funds or securities that managing,
tracking, and understanding the portfolio becomes difficult, without a meaningful
additional reduction in risk beyond a certain point.

Academic research generally suggests that diversification benefits diminish significantly
beyond a certain number of holdings; beyond that point, adding more holdings mainly adds
complexity rather than meaningfully reducing risk.

### Rebalancing

**Rebalancing** - periodically adjusting a portfolio back toward its originally intended
asset allocation (introduced in Unit 7.2), since different asset classes grow at
different rates over time, causing the actual proportions to drift from the original
plan.

1. Set a target asset allocation, based on your risk profile and goals (Units 7.1 and
   7.2).
2. Periodically review the portfolio's actual current allocation.
3. If the actual allocation has drifted meaningfully from the target, buy or sell to bring
   it back in line.
4. Repeat this process on a chosen schedule or trigger, as described below.

### Rebalancing methods

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| Calendar-based rebalancing | Rebalance at fixed intervals, such as once a year, regardless of how far allocation has drifted |
| Threshold-based rebalancing | Rebalance only when actual allocation drifts beyond a specified percentage from the target |
| Cash-flow-based rebalancing | Direct new investments (such as ongoing SIPs, Unit 4.1) toward the underweighted asset class, rather than selling existing holdings |

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Deepika started with a 60% equity, 40% debt allocation. After two strong years for
equities, her portfolio has drifted.

| Item | Target allocation | Actual allocation after drift (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Equity | 60% | 72% |
| Debt | 40% | 28% |

Illustrative, not a forecast. Using threshold-based rebalancing with a 10 percentage point
trigger, Deepika would sell some equity and add to debt, bringing the allocation back
toward 60:40, reducing her portfolio's risk back in line with her original risk profile.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Never rebalancing, allowing a portfolio to drift far from the originally intended risk
  level.
- Rebalancing too frequently, incurring unnecessary transaction costs and, where
  applicable, exit loads (Unit 3.3) or taxes (Chapter 8).
- Rebalancing based on short-term market emotion, rather than a predefined method or
  schedule.
- Confusing rebalancing with simply chasing whichever asset class has recently performed
  best.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Rebalancing that involves selling investments can trigger exit loads or capital gains tax,
covered in Chapter 8. Consider these costs when deciding how often and by how much to
rebalance, and consider directing new investments toward underweighted assets as a
lower-cost alternative where practical.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Prasanna Chandra's *Investment Analysis and Portfolio Management* and standard portfolio
theory texts describe diversification's risk-reduction benefit as coming specifically
from combining assets whose returns are not perfectly correlated, meaning they do not
always move in the same direction by the same amount. Rebalancing is described as a
discipline that manages a portfolio's risk level over time, since, without it, a
portfolio's actual risk can silently increase or decrease well beyond an investor's
originally intended risk profile, purely due to differing growth rates across asset
classes.
:::

## Quick recap

- Diversification spreads investments so no single holding dominates overall outcomes.
- Overlap between funds can undermine diversification, even when the number of funds held
  looks diversified on paper.
- Over-diversification adds complexity without meaningfully reducing risk beyond a certain
  point.
- Rebalancing periodically brings a portfolio back to its intended allocation, using
  calendar-based, threshold-based, or cash-flow-based methods.

## Check yourself

1. Why can holding five different large-cap funds still result in poor diversification?
   ::: details Show answer
   Because large-cap funds often hold many of the same top companies, creating overlap that
   reduces the real diversification benefit, even though it looks diversified on paper.
   :::
2. What is over-diversification?
   ::: details Show answer
   Holding so many funds or securities that the portfolio becomes hard to manage and
   understand, without a meaningful further reduction in risk beyond a certain point.
   :::
3. What is the purpose of rebalancing a portfolio?
   ::: details Show answer
   To periodically bring a portfolio's actual asset allocation back in line with its
   originally intended target, since different asset classes grow at different rates over
   time.
   :::
4. What is one lower-cost alternative to selling holdings when rebalancing?
   ::: details Show answer
   Directing new investments, such as ongoing SIP installments, toward the underweighted
   asset class, rather than selling existing holdings and potentially triggering costs or
   taxes.
   :::
