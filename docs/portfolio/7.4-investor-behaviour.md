# Investor Behaviour

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What loss aversion, herding, FOMO and overconfidence mean
- How each of these biases can lead to poor investment decisions
- Why market crashes are especially prone to triggering these biases
- Practical ways to stay disciplined during volatile periods
:::

## In plain words

Think of a classroom during a fire drill. Some students panic and rush the exit even when
there is no real danger, while others calmly follow the practised routine. Investing
during a market fall can feel similar: emotional reactions often push people toward hasty
decisions, while a calm, pre-planned routine tends to serve investors better.

## How it works

### Loss aversion

**Loss aversion** - the tendency to feel the pain of a loss more strongly than the
pleasure of an equivalent gain, leading investors to make decisions aimed more at avoiding
losses than at maximising expected long-term outcomes. This was introduced briefly in Unit
6.5.

In practice, loss aversion can cause investors to sell winning investments too early,
locking in a small gain, while holding onto losing investments too long, hoping to "get
back to even" before selling.

### Herding

**Herding** - following the actions or opinions of a crowd, such as buying a stock because
many others are buying it, rather than making an independent, reasoned decision.

Herding can amplify both rallies and crashes, since widespread buying or selling based on
others' actions, rather than independent analysis, can push prices further from what
fundamentals, introduced in Unit 5.4, would otherwise suggest.

### FOMO

**FOMO** (Fear Of Missing Out) - the anxiety of missing a potential gain that others appear
to be capturing, often leading to hurried investment decisions, particularly when an
asset's price has already risen sharply.

### Overconfidence

**Overconfidence** - overestimating one's own knowledge, skill, or ability to predict
market movements, introduced briefly in Unit 6.5, often leading to excessive trading,
inadequate diversification, or underestimating risk.

### How these biases interact during a market crash

| Bias | Typical behaviour during a crash |
|---|---|
| Loss aversion | Holding onto falling investments too long, hoping for recovery, or panic-selling near the bottom out of acute pain from paper losses |
| Herding | Selling because everyone else appears to be selling, reinforcing the downward move |
| FOMO (in reverse, sometimes called panic) | Fear of "missing the exit" before losses grow further, prompting a hurried sale |
| Overconfidence | Believing one can accurately time re-entry into the market after exiting, which is difficult even for professionals |

### Staying disciplined in crashes

1. Revisit your original goals, time horizon, and asset allocation (Units 7.1 and 7.2),
   set before the crash, rather than making decisions based on current emotion.
2. Avoid checking portfolio values excessively during periods of high volatility, since
   frequent checking can amplify emotional reactions.
3. Continue planned, regular investments, such as an ongoing SIP (Unit 4.1), if it still
   fits your goals, rather than pausing purely out of fear.
4. If considering a genuine change, base it on a reassessment of goals or risk capacity,
   not solely on recent market movements.
5. Consult a SEBI-registered investment adviser for significant decisions, rather than
   relying on social media sentiment or informal tips.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
During a sharp market fall, two investors react differently.

| Investor | Reaction | Likely outcome pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Naveen | Panics, redeems his equity fund at a loss, based on herding and acute loss aversion | Locks in the loss, and may miss a subsequent recovery |
| Reema | Reviews her original goals and time horizon, continues her SIP, and does not redeem | Remains invested according to her original plan, participating in any subsequent recovery |

This is illustrative, not a forecast or a guarantee of any specific outcome. It shows how
staying anchored to a pre-set plan, rather than reacting emotionally, is a central idea in
managing investor behaviour.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Making investment decisions based on recent price movements alone, rather than original
  goals and plans.
- Checking portfolio values so frequently that short-term volatility feels more
  significant than it actually is for a long-term goal.
- Buying an investment purely because it has risen sharply and is widely discussed, out of
  FOMO.
- Assuming you can reliably time exiting before a fall and re-entering before a recovery.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Investment decisions made under acute emotional stress, particularly during a sharp market
fall or a rapid price rise, are more prone to bias-driven mistakes. If you feel a strong
urge to make a large, sudden change, consider pausing before acting, and revisiting your
original plan or consulting a SEBI-registered investment adviser first.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Behavioural finance, a field referenced in Bodie, Kane and Marcus's *Investments*,
studies systematic deviations from purely rational decision-making in financial contexts.
Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's prospect theory, a foundational work in this field,
formally describes loss aversion as a core element of how people evaluate gains and
losses asymmetrically. NISM investment adviser workbooks reference these behavioural
biases as a key reason financial planning frameworks emphasise having a written,
pre-committed plan, rather than relying on in-the-moment judgement during volatile
periods.
:::

## Quick recap

- Loss aversion makes losses feel more painful than equivalent gains feel pleasurable,
  distorting decisions.
- Herding means following the crowd rather than independent analysis, which can amplify
  both rallies and crashes.
- FOMO can push hurried decisions to chase an asset that has already risen sharply.
- Overconfidence can lead to excessive trading or underestimating risk.
- Staying disciplined during a crash generally means returning to your original goals and
  plan, rather than reacting to short-term emotion.

## Check yourself

1. How can loss aversion lead to holding a losing investment too long?
   ::: details Show answer
   Because the pain of realising a loss by selling feels stronger than the equivalent
   pleasure of a similar-sized gain, investors often delay selling, hoping to "get back to
   even" first.
   :::
2. What is herding, and how can it amplify a market crash?
   ::: details Show answer
   Herding is following the crowd's actions rather than independent analysis. During a
   crash, widespread selling driven by herding can push prices down further than
   fundamentals alone would suggest.
   :::
3. Why is checking portfolio values very frequently during volatile periods generally
   discouraged?
   ::: details Show answer
   Frequent checking can amplify emotional reactions to short-term price swings, making
   long-term goals feel more threatened by short-term volatility than they actually are.
   :::
4. What is a practical, disciplined response to a sharp market fall, for someone with a
   long-term goal and appropriate asset allocation already in place?
   ::: details Show answer
   Revisiting the original goals and plan, avoiding decisions based purely on current
   emotion, and generally continuing planned investments such as an ongoing SIP if they
   still fit the original goals.
   :::
