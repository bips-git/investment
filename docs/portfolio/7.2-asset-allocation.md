# Asset Allocation

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

:::

## In plain words

Think of a balanced school timetable: some periods for core subjects, some for sports, and
some for arts, in proportions that suit a student's goals and strengths. **Asset
allocation** works the same way for money: deciding what proportion goes into different
types of investments, rather than putting everything into just one type.

## How it works

### The core idea

**Asset allocation** - dividing an investment portfolio across different asset classes
(such as equity, debt, gold, and real estate, introduced in Unit 1.4), in proportions
chosen to balance expected return and risk, based on goals, time horizon, and risk profile.

Different asset classes tend to behave differently under the same market conditions;
combining them in a deliberate proportion is intended to manage overall portfolio risk,
rather than relying on any single asset class alone.

### Risk profile

**Risk profile** - a combined assessment of an investor's risk appetite and risk capacity,
both introduced in Unit 1.3, used to guide how much equity, debt, and other assets suit
that investor.

| Risk profile | General tendency |
|---|---|
| Conservative | Prioritises capital safety, generally a lower equity proportion |
| Moderate | Balances growth and safety, a mixed equity-debt proportion |
| Aggressive | Prioritises long-term growth, generally a higher equity proportion, given adequate risk capacity |

### Illustrative allocations by life stage

| Life stage | Illustrative equity proportion | Illustrative debt and other proportion | General reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early career (20s-30s) | Higher, for example 70-80% | Lower, for example 20-30% | Longer time horizon to manage equity volatility (Unit 1.3) |
| Mid-career (40s) | Moderate, for example 50-60% | Moderate, for example 40-50% | Balancing growth with increasing near-term responsibilities |
| Approaching retirement (50s-60s) | Lower, for example 30-40% | Higher, for example 60-70% | Shorter time horizon, reduced capacity to absorb a downturn |
| Retired | Lower, for example 20-30% | Higher, for example 70-80% | Emphasis on stability and regular income over growth |

These percentages are illustrative, not a forecast or a personalised recommendation. Actual
allocation should depend on individual goals, risk profile, and circumstances, and can
reasonably differ from this general pattern.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
This general life-stage pattern is a widely used illustrative framework, not a fixed
regulatory rule. For a personalised allocation, consider consulting a SEBI-registered
investment adviser, since individual circumstances vary significantly.
:::

### Why allocation matters more than individual picks

Academic and industry research, referenced in standard portfolio management texts, has
found that the proportion allocated across asset classes tends to explain a large part of
a portfolio's overall variation in returns over time, often more than the specific
individual securities chosen within each asset class.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Two investors in their early 30s, each with ₹10,00,000 to invest, choose different
allocations based on differing risk profiles.

| Investor | Risk profile | Illustrative allocation |
|---|---|---|
| Ramesh | Aggressive, high risk capacity | 80% equity, 20% debt |
| Sunita | Moderate, lower risk capacity due to a large upcoming expense | 50% equity, 50% debt |

This is illustrative, not a forecast or a recommendation. Both are in the same life stage,
but their different risk profiles and circumstances lead to a different, individually
appropriate allocation.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Choosing an allocation based only on age, without considering personal risk profile and
  specific goals.
- Chasing an allocation that worked for someone else, without checking if the underlying
  circumstances match.
- Setting an allocation once and never reviewing it as life stage or goals change.
- Confusing asset allocation with simply picking a few popular individual stocks or funds.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
An asset allocation with high equity exposure but low risk capacity, for example, someone
close to needing the money for an inflexible near-term expense, can be genuinely
mismatched, regardless of how comfortable that person feels with volatility. Risk capacity,
not just risk appetite, should meaningfully guide the final allocation, as discussed in
Unit 1.3.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Modern Portfolio Theory, developed by Harry Markowitz and covered in Bodie, Kane and
Marcus's *Investments*, formalises asset allocation as a way to construct a portfolio that
seeks the best possible expected return for a given level of risk, by combining assets
whose returns do not move in perfect lockstep with each other. This is closely related to
diversification, covered further in Unit 7.3, though asset allocation specifically refers
to the broad split across asset classes, while diversification often refers to spreading
investments within an asset class as well.
:::

## Quick recap

- Asset allocation divides a portfolio across asset classes, balancing expected return and
  risk.
- Risk profile, combining risk appetite and risk capacity, should guide an appropriate
  allocation.
- Illustrative life-stage patterns generally shift from higher equity earlier to higher
  debt later, though individual circumstances vary.
- Research suggests allocation across asset classes explains much of a portfolio's overall
  return variation, more than individual security selection.
