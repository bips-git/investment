# Types of Funds

## In plain words

Think of a school offering different streams: science, commerce, and arts, each suited to
different student interests and career paths. Mutual funds work similarly. SEBI requires
every scheme to fit into a defined category, so investors can compare similar funds fairly,
instead of facing confusing, overlapping fund names.

## How it works

### SEBI's broad fund categories

SEBI's mutual fund categorisation rules group schemes into broad categories, mainly based
on what they invest in: **equity funds** (mainly shares), **debt funds** (mainly bonds and
money market instruments), **hybrid funds** (a mix of equity and debt), and other
categories such as **solution-oriented** and **index or ETF** funds, covered further in
Unit 3.4.

### Equity fund sub-types: cap-based categories

Equity funds are further split based on **market capitalisation** (market cap) - the total
market value of a company's shares, introduced in Chapter 5.

| Equity fund type | What it mainly invests in |
|---|---|
| Large-cap fund | Larger, more established companies |
| Mid-cap fund | Medium-sized companies, generally more volatile than large-cap |
| Small-cap fund | Smaller companies, generally the most volatile of the three |
| Flexi-cap fund | A mix of large, mid and small-cap companies, at the fund manager's discretion |

Generally, small-cap funds carry higher volatility (introduced in Unit 1.3) than large-cap
funds, though this is a general pattern, illustrative and not a forecast for any specific
fund or period.

### Debt funds

**Debt funds** invest mainly in bonds, government securities, and money market
instruments. They generally aim for more stable, moderate returns than equity funds, but
still carry interest rate risk (bond prices can fall when interest rates rise) and credit
risk (introduced in Unit 1.3), depending on the quality of borrowers held.

### Hybrid funds

**Hybrid funds** invest in a mix of equity and debt, in varying proportions depending on
the specific scheme's stated strategy, aiming to balance growth potential with some
stability.

### Liquid funds

**Liquid funds** - a type of debt fund that invests in very short-term money market
instruments, aiming for high liquidity and low volatility. They are often used for
emergency funds or short-term parking of money, referenced in Unit 1.4.

### ELSS

**ELSS** (Equity Linked Savings Scheme) - an equity mutual fund category that offers tax
benefits under the Income Tax Act, but comes with a mandatory lock-in period. Tax rules
for investments are covered in full in Chapter 8.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
ELSS lock-in periods and applicable tax benefit limits are set by tax law and can change.
Confirm current rules on the Income Tax Department's website before relying on them for
tax planning.
:::

### Comparing categories at a glance

| Category | Typical risk (see Unit 1.3) | Typical time horizon | Common use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large-cap equity | Moderate to high | 5 years or more | Long-term growth with relatively lower equity volatility |
| Mid-cap / small-cap equity | High | 7 years or more | Long-term growth, higher volatility |
| Flexi-cap equity | Varies with allocation | 5 years or more | Long-term growth with flexible allocation |
| Debt fund | Low to moderate | Varies by scheme duration | Relatively stable income, shorter to medium horizons |
| Hybrid fund | Moderate | 3 to 5 years or more | Balance of growth and stability |
| Liquid fund | Low | Days to a few months | Emergency fund, short-term parking |
| ELSS | High (equity-based) | Minimum lock-in, ideally longer | Tax-saving with equity growth potential |

This table shows general, typical patterns. It is illustrative, not a forecast or a
recommendation of any specific scheme.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Priya is planning three different goals.

| Goal | Time horizon | Category that generally fits the pattern above |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency fund | Immediate | Liquid fund |
| Home down payment | 5 years | Hybrid or large-cap equity fund |
| Retirement | 25 years | Diversified equity, such as flexi-cap |

This matching is illustrative, not a forecast or a recommendation of any specific scheme.
It only shows how time horizon can guide which broad category may suit a goal, following
the same logic introduced in Unit 1.4.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Choosing a small-cap fund for a short-term goal, ignoring its higher typical volatility.
- Investing in ELSS purely for tax saving, without checking the lock-in period fits the
  goal.
- Assuming "debt fund" always means zero risk; interest rate and credit risk still apply.
- Not checking a scheme's actual SEBI category before comparing it with other funds.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Fund names can sound similar to their category, but always check the scheme document's
stated SEBI category, not just the name, before investing. Some fund names can be
marketed attractively while the actual underlying holdings differ from what the name
suggests.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
SEBI's mutual fund categorisation circular, widely referenced in NISM workbooks, defines
precise market capitalisation ranges for large-cap, mid-cap and small-cap companies, and
restricts each equity fund category to a defined minimum allocation matching its category.
This standardisation was introduced specifically to reduce confusing overlap between
similarly named schemes across different AMCs, supporting fair comparison, a concept
Prasanna Chandra's *Investment Analysis and Portfolio Management* frames as essential for
informed investor decision-making.
:::

## Quick recap

- SEBI groups mutual funds into broad categories: equity, debt, hybrid, and others.
- Equity funds are further split by market cap: large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap and
  flexi-cap.
- Debt funds invest mainly in bonds and money market instruments; liquid funds are a very
  short-term, low-volatility debt fund type.
- Hybrid funds mix equity and debt; ELSS is an equity category with tax benefits and a
  lock-in.
- Matching a fund category to your goal's time horizon follows the same logic as Unit 1.4.

## Check yourself

1. What generally differs between a large-cap fund and a small-cap fund?
   ::: details Show answer
   A large-cap fund mainly invests in larger, more established companies, generally with
   lower volatility. A small-cap fund invests in smaller companies, generally with higher
   volatility.
   :::
2. Why might a liquid fund suit an emergency fund better than a small-cap equity fund?
   ::: details Show answer
   Liquid funds aim for high liquidity and low volatility, matching the need for quick,
   stable access to money. Small-cap funds carry higher volatility, less suitable for money
   that may be needed urgently.
   :::
3. What is the key trade-off with an ELSS fund's tax benefit?
   ::: details Show answer
   ELSS offers tax benefits under the Income Tax Act, but comes with a mandatory lock-in
   period during which the investment cannot be redeemed.
   :::
4. Why should you check a scheme's stated SEBI category rather than relying on its name
   alone?
   ::: details Show answer
   Fund names can be marketed attractively while not fully reflecting the actual category or
   underlying holdings. The stated SEBI category gives a standardised, comparable
   classification.
   :::
