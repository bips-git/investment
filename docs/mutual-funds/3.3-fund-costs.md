# Fund Costs

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What an expense ratio is and why it matters every single year
- How direct and regular plans differ in cost, in more detail than Unit 2.4
- What an exit load is and when it applies
- How small cost differences compound into large amounts over 20 years
:::

## In plain words

Think of two identical school buses running the exact same route every day. One driver
charges a small extra fee for fuel and maintenance each month; another charges a bit more.
Over a single month, the difference feels small. Over many years, that small extra fee,
paid again and again, adds up to a large amount. Mutual fund costs work the same way.

## How it works

### Expense ratio

**Expense ratio** - the annual fee a mutual fund charges, as a percentage of the
scheme's assets, to cover fund management, administration and distribution costs. It is
deducted from the scheme's assets before NAV (introduced in Unit 3.1) is calculated,
so it reduces returns automatically, without a separate bill being sent to you.

```
expense ratio impact per year ≈ expense ratio % x amount invested
```

### Direct vs regular plans, revisited

Unit 2.4 introduced direct and regular plans. In direct plans, there is no distributor
commission, so the expense ratio is lower. In regular plans, the expense ratio includes
the distributor's commission, making it higher.

| Plan type | Illustrative expense ratio (equity fund) | Illustrative expense ratio (debt fund) |
|---|---|---|
| Direct | 0.8% per year | 0.3% per year |
| Regular | 1.5% per year | 0.8% per year |

These figures are illustrative, not a forecast. Actual expense ratios vary by scheme, fund
size, and category, and are disclosed in each scheme's factsheet, covered in Unit 3.5.

### Exit load

**Exit load** - a fee charged when units are redeemed (sold back) before a specified
minimum holding period, usually expressed as a percentage of the redemption amount.

1. Check the scheme document or factsheet for the exit load period, commonly around one
   year for many equity funds, though this varies by scheme.
2. If units are redeemed before that period ends, the exit load percentage is deducted
   from the redemption amount.
3. If units are redeemed after that period, no exit load usually applies.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
Exit load percentages and holding periods differ by scheme and can change over time.
Always confirm the current exit load structure in the specific scheme's factsheet before
investing or redeeming.
:::

### The 20-year cost impact

A seemingly small difference in expense ratio compounds over long periods, in the same way
compounding builds returns, as covered in Unit 1.2.

| Detail | Direct plan (0.8% expense ratio) | Regular plan (1.5% expense ratio) |
|---|---|---|
| Lump sum invested | ₹5,00,000 | ₹5,00,000 |
| Assumed gross annual return before expenses | 12% | 12% |
| Approximate net annual return after expenses | 11.2% | 10.5% |
| Approximate value after 20 years | ₹41,00,000 | ₹36,60,000 |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast, and assume the same gross return for
simplicity. The approximately 0.7 percentage point difference in expense ratio results in
a difference of several lakh rupees over 20 years, purely from costs, even though both
plans could hold the exact same underlying investments.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Two investors, Anita and Bilal, invest ₹5,00,000 each in the same scheme, one in a direct
plan and one in a regular plan, illustrated in the table above.

Anita, in the direct plan, ends up with roughly ₹4,40,000 more than Bilal after 20 years,
purely because of the lower expense ratio, assuming both plans deliver the same underlying
gross performance. This is illustrative, not a forecast, but it shows why the choice
between direct and regular plans matters over long horizons.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Comparing two funds only on past returns, without checking their expense ratios.
- Redeeming units just before a milestone, without checking whether an exit load applies.
- Assuming a 0.5 or 0.7 percentage point expense ratio difference is "too small to
  matter".
- Not checking whether an app is offering a direct or a regular plan by default.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Distributors earning a commission from regular plans have a financial incentive to
recommend regular plans over direct plans. This does not make regular plans dishonest, but
it is worth being aware of this incentive, and comparing the actual expense ratio
disclosed in the factsheet, before deciding.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
SEBI caps the maximum expense ratio a mutual fund scheme can charge, on a sliding scale
based on AUM (introduced in Unit 3.1), with lower caps for larger schemes. This is
described in SEBI's Mutual Funds Regulations and referenced in NISM workbooks. John
Bogle, founder of Vanguard, argued extensively in his writings that costs are one of the
few factors an investor can control with certainty, since future returns are uncertain but
expense ratios are known and compound negatively in the same mathematical way that
positive returns compound, as shown in Unit 1.2.
:::

## Quick recap

- Expense ratio is the annual fee, as a percentage of assets, deducted before NAV is
  calculated.
- Direct plans have a lower expense ratio than regular plans, since they skip distributor
  commission.
- Exit load is a fee for redeeming units before a scheme's specified minimum holding
  period.
- Small expense ratio differences compound into large amounts over long periods, such as
  20 years.
- Always check a scheme's factsheet for its exact expense ratio and exit load structure.

## Check yourself

1. When is the expense ratio actually deducted from your investment?
   ::: details Show answer
   It is deducted from the scheme's assets before NAV is calculated, so it reduces returns
   automatically, without a separate bill.
   :::
2. Why do regular plans usually have a higher expense ratio than direct plans?
   ::: details Show answer
   Regular plans include the distributor's commission built into the expense ratio, while
   direct plans skip the distributor and do not carry that commission.
   :::
3. What happens if you redeem units before a scheme's exit load period ends?
   ::: details Show answer
   An exit load percentage is deducted from the redemption amount, as a fee for redeeming
   early.
   :::
4. Why does even a small expense ratio difference, such as 0.7 percentage points, matter so
   much over 20 years?
   ::: details Show answer
   Because expense ratios reduce returns every single year, and this effect compounds over
   time in the same mathematical way that positive returns compound, leading to a large
   difference over long periods.
   :::
