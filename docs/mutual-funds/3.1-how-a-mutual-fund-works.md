# How a Mutual Fund Works

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- How a mutual fund pools money from many investors
- What units, NAV and AUM actually mean

## In plain words

Think of a school van service. Instead of every parent buying and running a separate car,
many families pool money together to fund one van, driven by a trained driver, who picks
the best route for everyone. A **mutual fund** works the same way - it pools money from
many investors and invests it together, managed by a professional, according to a stated
strategy.

## How it works

### Pooling and units

When many investors put money into the same mutual fund scheme, their money is pooled
together. Each investor receives **units** - small, equal shares of the total pooled
investment - in proportion to the amount they invested.

### NAV: the price of one unit

**NAV** (Net Asset Value) - the price of one unit of a mutual fund on a given day, equal
to the value of all the scheme's investments, minus its expenses, divided by the total
number of units outstanding.

```
NAV = (value of all investments held - expenses) / total units outstanding
```

NAV changes daily, since the value of the underlying investments changes daily.

### AUM: the size of the fund

**AUM** (Assets Under Management) - the total value of all investments a fund, or a fund
manager, currently manages. AUM is often used as one measure of a scheme's or an AMC's
size, though size alone does not indicate quality of management.

### The fund manager

A **fund manager** is the professional, employed by the AMC (introduced in Unit 2.1), who
decides which shares, bonds or other instruments the scheme actually buys and sells, based
on the scheme's stated objective and strategy.

### Open-ended vs closed-ended funds

| Type | Buying and selling units | Typical liquidity |
|---|---|---|
| Open-ended fund | Can be bought or sold (redeemed) on most business days, at that day's NAV | High |
| Closed-ended fund | Units can be bought only during a limited launch period, then usually trade on an exchange | Lower, depends on exchange trading activity |

Most mutual funds available to retail investors in India are open-ended.

### SEBI safeguards behind every mutual fund

1. Every mutual fund scheme is created under a trust structure, with trustees (introduced
   in Unit 2.1) overseeing the AMC.
2. The AMC must follow SEBI's mutual fund regulations, covering what a scheme can invest
   in and how it must be run.
3. Every scheme has a scheme document, disclosing its objective, risks, and costs.
4. NAV must be calculated and published according to SEBI's rules, so investors know the
   fair value of their units each day.
5. A custodian, a separate institution, physically holds the securities a scheme owns, so
   the AMC never directly controls the underlying assets.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
An equity mutual fund scheme has ₹100 crore in total assets after expenses, and 10 crore
units outstanding.

| Item | Value (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Total scheme assets, after expenses | ₹100 crore |
| Total units outstanding | 10 crore |
| NAV per unit | ₹10.00 |

₹100 crore means ₹100,00,00,000, using the Indian numbering system where 1 crore equals
1,00,00,000. If Kavita invests ₹10,000 at this NAV, she receives 1,000 units
(₹10,000 divided by ₹10.00 per unit), illustrative and not a forecast.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Believing a lower NAV means a "cheaper" or "better value" fund; NAV alone says nothing
  about future performance.
- Confusing AUM (fund size) with fund quality or expected return.
- Assuming all mutual funds work like closed-ended funds, with limited buying windows.
- Not reading the scheme document before investing, especially its stated objective and
  risks.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
A mutual fund's structure, with trustees, a custodian, and SEBI oversight, is designed so
that even if an AMC faces trouble, investors' underlying securities remain separately held
and protected. This does not mean mutual funds are risk-free; market risk, covered in Unit
1.3, still applies to the value of the investments themselves.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Prasanna Chandra's *Investment Analysis and Portfolio Management* and standard NISM mutual
fund workbooks describe the mutual fund structure as a trust, with three separate parties:
the sponsor (who sets up the fund), trustees (who oversee it on investors' behalf), and the
AMC (who manages the investments day to day), with a custodian separately holding the
assets. This four-way separation of roles is a core investor protection mechanism required
under SEBI's Mutual Funds Regulations.
:::

## Quick recap

- A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it together.
- Units represent an investor's share of the pooled investment; NAV is the price of one
  unit.
- AUM measures the total size of assets a fund or AMC manages.
- A fund manager decides what the scheme actually buys and sells.
- Open-ended funds allow buying and selling on most business days; closed-ended funds do
  not.
- SEBI's trust structure, with trustees and a custodian, protects investors' underlying
  assets.

## Check yourself

1. If a scheme's NAV is ₹20 and you invest ₹2,000, how many units do you receive?
   ::: details Show answer
   100 units (₹2,000 divided by ₹20 per unit), illustrative and not a forecast.
   :::
2. Does a higher AUM mean a fund is a better investment?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. AUM measures the size of assets managed, not the quality of management or expected
   future returns.
   :::
3. What is the main liquidity difference between open-ended and closed-ended funds?
   ::: details Show answer
   Open-ended funds can usually be bought or sold on most business days at that day's NAV.
   Closed-ended funds can only be bought during a limited launch period, and are less liquid
   afterward.
   :::
4. Why does a custodian holding the assets separately from the AMC matter for investor
   protection?
   ::: details Show answer
   It means the AMC does not directly control the underlying securities, reducing the risk
   that trouble at the AMC would directly affect investors' actual holdings.
   :::

## 🔑 Keywords

| Term | Meaning in one line |
|---|---|
| Mutual fund | A pooled investment vehicle managed by professionals on behalf of many investors |
| Unit | A small, equal share of a mutual fund scheme's pooled investment |
| NAV (Net Asset Value) | The price of one unit of a mutual fund on a given day |
| AUM (Assets Under Management) | The total value of assets a fund or AMC currently manages |
| Fund manager | The professional who decides a scheme's actual buy and sell decisions |
| Open-ended fund | A fund that can be bought or sold on most business days at that day's NAV |
| Closed-ended fund | A fund with a limited buying window, less liquid afterward |
| Custodian | A separate institution that physically holds a scheme's securities |
