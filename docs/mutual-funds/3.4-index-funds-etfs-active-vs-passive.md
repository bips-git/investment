# Index Funds, ETFs, Active vs Passive

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What an index fund and an ETF are, and how they differ
- What tracking error means

- What the evidence and key thinkers say about active fund performance

## In plain words

Think of two ways to cook a well-known family recipe. One cook tries to improve it every
time, adding their own twist, hoping to make it taste even better than the original. The
other cook follows the original recipe exactly, every single time, without trying to
improve on it. Passive investing works like the second cook: it simply tries to copy a
market index, rather than trying to beat it.

## How it works

### What an index is

An **index**, such as the Nifty 50 or the Sensex, is a defined list of stocks, combined in
a specific way, used to represent and track the performance of a market or a segment of
it. Indices were introduced briefly in Unit 2.1 in the context of exchanges.

### Index funds

An **index fund** is a mutual fund designed to hold the same stocks, in roughly the same
proportion, as a specific market index, aiming to match that index's performance rather
than to beat it.

### ETFs

An **ETF** (Exchange Traded Fund) is similar to an index fund in aiming to track an index,
but it is bought and sold on a stock exchange, like a share, through a demat and trading
account (introduced in Unit 2.3), instead of through the mutual fund application process
described in Unit 2.2.

| Feature | Index fund | ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Bought through | Mutual fund application process (AMC, RTA, or app) | Stock exchange, using a demat and trading account |
| Priced | Once a day, at NAV | Throughout the trading day, at the exchange-traded price |
| Requires demat account | Not necessarily | Yes |

### Tracking error

**Tracking error** - the difference between an index fund's or ETF's actual returns and
the index's actual returns, over the same period. A lower tracking error means the fund is
more closely matching its target index.

### Active vs passive management

**Active management** - a fund manager actively selects stocks or bonds, aiming to beat a
benchmark index through research and judgement, as described for a general fund manager in
Unit 3.1. **Passive management** - a fund simply tracks an index, without trying to beat
it, as described above for index funds and ETFs.

| Approach | Goal | Typical expense ratio (see Unit 3.3) |
|---|---|---|
| Active | Beat the benchmark index | Generally higher |
| Passive | Match the benchmark index | Generally lower |

### Evidence on active fund performance

Multiple long-term studies, including SEBI and industry data, have found that a
significant share of actively managed equity funds underperform their benchmark index over
long periods, after accounting for costs. [VERIFY: check the latest SEBI or AMFI study on
active fund outperformance rates for the most current figures, since these percentages
change with each study period.]

Studies on the percentage of active funds beating their benchmark change with each new

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Rohan compares two ways to get exposure to India's largest companies.

| Detail | Active large-cap fund (illustrative) | Nifty 50 index fund (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Try to beat the Nifty 50 index | Match the Nifty 50 index |
| Illustrative expense ratio | 1.2% per year | 0.2% per year |
| Outcome over long periods | Varies; some years beat the index, some years lag | Closely tracks the index, minus a small tracking error and expense ratio |

This is illustrative, not a forecast or a recommendation of any specific fund. It shows
the basic trade-off: active management offers a chance to beat the index, but at a
generally higher cost and with uncertain outcomes; passive management offers a lower-cost
way to closely match the index.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Assuming all index funds tracking the same index perform identically; tracking error and
  expense ratio still cause small differences between them.
- Believing an ETF and an index fund are always interchangeable, without checking whether
  a demat account is required.
- Judging active vs passive purely on a single year's performance, instead of long-term
  patterns after costs.
- Ignoring that even passive funds carry market risk, introduced in Unit 1.3, since they
  still track markets that can fall.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Passive does not mean risk-free. An index fund or ETF can still lose value if the index it
tracks falls. The active vs passive choice is about management style and cost, not about
avoiding market risk.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
John Bogle, founder of Vanguard and a pioneer of low-cost index investing, argued in his
writings that most actively managed funds fail to consistently beat their benchmark after
costs, over long periods, making low-cost index funds a reasonable default for many
investors. Burton Malkiel, in *A Random Walk Down Wall Street*, argues along similar lines
using the efficient market hypothesis, the idea that stock prices already reflect available
public information, making it structurally difficult for active managers to consistently
find mispriced stocks. These are influential, debated positions in the field, not
universally agreed conclusions; other researchers and practitioners argue skilled active
managers can add value in specific market segments or periods.
:::

## Quick recap

- An index fund or ETF tries to match an index's performance, rather than beat it.
- ETFs trade on an exchange throughout the day; index funds are priced once daily at NAV.
- Tracking error measures how closely a fund follows its target index.
- Active management tries to beat a benchmark; passive management tries to match it, and
  the choice involves a cost and outcome-certainty trade-off.
- Passive investing still carries market risk; it does not remove risk, only management
  style differs.

## Check yourself

1. What is the main goal difference between an index fund and an actively managed fund?
   ::: details Show answer
   An index fund tries to match the performance of a specific market index. An actively
   managed fund tries to beat a benchmark index through the fund manager's stock or bond
   selection.
   :::
2. What is the key operational difference between an index fund and an ETF?
   ::: details Show answer
   An index fund is bought through the mutual fund application process, priced once daily at
   NAV. An ETF is bought and sold on a stock exchange throughout the trading day, requiring
   a demat and trading account.
   :::
3. What does a lower tracking error indicate?
   ::: details Show answer
   It indicates the fund is more closely matching the performance of its target index.
   :::
4. Does choosing a passive index fund remove market risk?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. A passive fund still tracks a market index, which can rise or fall. Passive investing
   changes the management style and typical cost, not the underlying market risk.
   :::
