# Reading a Stock Page

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What price and volume tell you about a stock
- What EPS and P/E ratio mean, and their limitations
- What book value and ROE indicate
- What debt-equity ratio and promoter holding reveal about a company
:::

## In plain words

Think of checking a student's overall school profile before deciding whether to recommend
them for a scholarship: not just their latest exam score, but their attendance,
consistency, and background. A stock page similarly gives a fuller profile of a company,
beyond just its current price.

## How it works

### Price and volume

**Price** is the most recently traded price of a share, as introduced in Unit 5.1.
**Volume** - the total number of shares traded during a given period, usually a single
trading day.

High volume alongside a price movement generally suggests stronger conviction behind that
move, while a price movement on unusually low volume may reflect less broad participation.

### EPS

**EPS** (Earnings Per Share) - a company's net profit divided by its total number of
outstanding shares, showing how much profit is attributable to each share.

```
EPS = net profit / total number of outstanding shares
```

### P/E ratio

**P/E ratio** (Price-to-Earnings ratio) - a company's current share price divided by its
EPS, commonly used to gauge how expensive a share is relative to its current earnings.

```
P/E ratio = share price / EPS
```

| Company | Share price | EPS | P/E ratio (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Company A | ₹200 | ₹10 | 20 |
| Company B | ₹500 | ₹50 | 10 |

This is illustrative, not a forecast or a recommendation. A higher P/E can mean the market
expects stronger future growth, or that the share is relatively expensive compared to
current earnings; a lower P/E can mean the opposite, or can reflect genuine business
concerns. P/E alone does not tell you which explanation applies.

### Book value

**Book value** - a company's total assets minus its total liabilities, representing the
net worth attributable to shareholders, often expressed on a per-share basis as book value
per share.

### ROE

**ROE** (Return on Equity) - a company's net profit divided by its shareholders' equity
(book value), expressed as a percentage, showing how efficiently a company generates
profit from shareholders' invested capital.

```
ROE = net profit / shareholders' equity x 100
```

### Debt-equity ratio

**Debt-equity ratio** - a company's total debt divided by its shareholders' equity,
indicating how much a company relies on borrowed money relative to its own capital.

```
debt-equity ratio = total debt / shareholders' equity
```

A higher debt-equity ratio generally means higher financial risk, since debt must be
repaid regardless of business performance, connecting to the credit risk concept
introduced in Unit 1.3.

### Promoter holding

**Promoter holding** - the percentage of a company's total shares held by its promoters
(the founders or controlling shareholders). Changes in promoter holding, particularly
promoters selling a large portion of their stake, are often watched closely by other
investors as one data point among several.

### Putting the numbers together

| Metric | What it broadly indicates |
|---|---|
| Price and volume | Current market value and trading activity |
| EPS | Profit attributable to each share |
| P/E ratio | How the market values earnings, relative to current profit |
| Book value | Net worth attributable to shareholders |
| ROE | How efficiently profit is generated from shareholders' capital |
| Debt-equity ratio | How much the company relies on borrowed money |
| Promoter holding | Level and change in founder or controlling ownership |

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Comparing two companies from the same industry, illustrative and not a recommendation.

| Metric | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Share price | ₹300 | ₹150 |
| EPS | ₹15 | ₹10 |
| P/E ratio | 20 | 15 |
| ROE | 18% | 12% |
| Debt-equity ratio | 0.4 | 1.2 |
| Promoter holding | 55% | 40% |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. Company A shows higher ROE and lower debt,
which some investors might view favourably, but its P/E is also higher, meaning the market
is pricing in more expectation relative to current profit. Reading a stock page means
weighing several such metrics together, not picking just one.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Judging a stock only by its P/E ratio, without considering ROE, debt levels, and
  business quality together.
- Assuming a "low" P/E always means a bargain, without checking why it is low.
- Ignoring debt-equity ratio, especially in industries sensitive to interest rate changes.
- Treating promoter holding changes as automatically negative, without checking the actual
  reason behind the change.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
No single ratio on a stock page tells the whole story about a company. Relying on one
number alone, especially from a tip shared on social media or a messaging group, without
independently checking other metrics and the company's disclosures, is a common way
investors make poor decisions. Unregistered tip-sharing is covered further in Chapter 9.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Fundamental analysis, as described in Prasanna Chandra's *Investment Analysis and
Portfolio Management* and Graham's value investing writings, evaluates a company using
combinations of profitability ratios (like ROE), valuation ratios (like P/E), and
solvency ratios (like debt-equity), rather than any single ratio in isolation. Graham, in
particular, emphasised buying with a margin of safety, using such fundamentals to estimate
a company's intrinsic value rather than relying purely on current market price.
:::

## Quick recap

- Price and volume together show a stock's current value and level of trading activity.
- EPS shows profit per share; P/E ratio shows how the market values that profit.
- Book value shows shareholders' net worth in the company; ROE shows how efficiently that
  capital generates profit.
- Debt-equity ratio shows reliance on borrowed money; promoter holding shows founder or
  controlling ownership level.
- No single metric should be used alone; reading a stock page means weighing several
  metrics together.

## Check yourself

1. What does the P/E ratio broadly indicate?
   ::: details Show answer
   How the market values a company's share price relative to its current earnings per
   share; a higher P/E can reflect either growth expectations or relative expensiveness.
   :::
2. What does a higher debt-equity ratio generally suggest about a company?
   ::: details Show answer
   It suggests the company relies more on borrowed money relative to its own capital,
   generally indicating higher financial risk.
   :::
3. Why is ROE useful alongside a company's profit figures?
   ::: details Show answer
   ROE shows how efficiently a company generates profit relative to shareholders' invested
   capital, rather than just the absolute profit amount.
   :::
4. Why should you avoid relying on a single metric, like P/E alone, to judge a stock?
   ::: details Show answer
   A single metric does not capture the full picture; ROE, debt levels, promoter holding and
   other factors together give a more complete, balanced view of a company.
   :::
