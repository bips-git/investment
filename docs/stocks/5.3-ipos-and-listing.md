# IPOs and Listing

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What an IPO is and why companies do them
- How to apply for an IPO using UPI
- How allotment and lot size work
- What listing risk is, and how SME IPOs differ from mainboard IPOs
:::

## In plain words

Think of a popular school play with a limited number of seats. Interested families apply
for tickets in advance, but not everyone who applies is guaranteed a seat, since demand can
exceed supply. Buying shares through an **IPO** works in a similar way: many investors can
apply, but only some receive an allotment, depending on demand.

## How it works

### What an IPO is

**IPO** (Initial Public Offering) - the process through which a private company offers its
shares to the public for the first time, becoming a listed company on a stock exchange
(introduced in Unit 2.1). Companies typically raise an IPO to raise funds, and to allow
early investors an opportunity to sell some of their holding.

### Applying through UPI

Retail investors in India typically apply for an IPO using their **UPI** (Unified
Payments Interface) ID, through their broker's app.

1. Select the IPO and enter the number of lots and bid price within the specified price
   band.
2. The application amount is blocked (not debited) in your bank account, using a UPI
   mandate.
3. If shares are allotted, the blocked amount is debited; if not allotted, the block is
   released, and no money is deducted.

### Lot size

**Lot size** - the minimum number of shares an investor must apply for in an IPO, set by
the company for that specific issue. Retail investors can typically apply for one lot, or
in multiples of one lot, up to a specified limit.

### Allotment

**Allotment** - the process of assigning IPO shares to applicants, based on demand and
SEBI's allotment rules. If an IPO is oversubscribed (more applications than shares
available), not all applicants receive shares; allotment for retail investors is often
done through a computerised lottery process in oversubscribed issues.

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| 1 | You apply for a chosen number of lots, with the amount blocked via UPI mandate |
| 2 | The company and its registrar determine total demand across all applicants |
| 3 | If undersubscribed or exactly subscribed, all valid applicants are usually allotted their applied lots |
| 4 | If oversubscribed, allotment is often decided through a computerised lottery, especially for retail investors |
| 5 | Allotted shares are credited to your demat account before listing |

### Listing and listing risk

**Listing** - the day a company's shares begin trading on a stock exchange for the first
time, after the IPO process is complete. **Listing risk** - the risk that a share's price
on its listing day, sometimes called the listing price, is significantly higher or lower
than the price at which it was issued in the IPO, based on how the market values the
company on listing day.

::: warning ⚠️ Listing day price movements can be significant
A share can list well above or well below its IPO price, based on market demand on the
listing day. This movement is not guaranteed in either direction, and past patterns for
other IPOs do not predict a specific IPO's listing outcome.
:::

### SME IPOs

**SME IPO** - an IPO by a smaller company, listed on a dedicated SME (Small and Medium
Enterprises) platform of an exchange, generally under different (often less strict)
disclosure and eligibility norms than mainboard IPOs, and often with lower liquidity
after listing.

| Feature | Mainboard IPO | SME IPO |
|---|---|---|
| Typical company size | Larger, established companies | Smaller companies |
| Minimum investment (lot size value) | Generally lower | Generally higher |
| Typical post-listing liquidity | Generally higher | Generally lower |
| Regulatory and disclosure norms | Standard mainboard norms | Separate SME platform norms |

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
Exact IPO eligibility norms, lot sizes, and SME platform rules are set by SEBI and
exchanges, and can change. Confirm current norms on the SEBI or NSE and BSE websites
before applying to any specific IPO.
:::

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Rakesh applies for an IPO with a lot size of 50 shares, priced between ₹95 and ₹100 per
share.

| Step | Detail (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Application | 1 lot (50 shares) at the cut-off price |
| Amount blocked via UPI mandate | ₹5,000 (50 shares x ₹100) |
| Outcome if allotted | ₹5,000 debited, 50 shares credited to demat account before listing |
| Outcome if not allotted | Block released, no amount debited |

This is illustrative, not a forecast or a recommendation to apply to any specific IPO.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Assuming an IPO application guarantees an allotment, especially for oversubscribed
  issues.
- Not distinguishing between mainboard and SME IPOs, and their different liquidity and
  risk profiles.
- Assuming a share will always list above its IPO price; listing risk can work in either
  direction.
- Forgetting to check that the UPI mandate approval request actually matches the intended
  IPO application.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Only approve UPI mandate requests that you can verify match your own IPO application,
through your broker's official app. Never approve a mandate request you do not recognise
or did not personally initiate.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
SEBI's ICDR (Issue of Capital and Disclosure Requirements) Regulations govern IPO
disclosure, pricing, and allotment processes, including reserved categories for retail
investors, and specific, separate norms for the SME platform. NISM workbooks describe the
retail allotment lottery mechanism as designed to give smaller applicants a fair,
randomised chance in oversubscribed issues, rather than favouring larger applications.
:::

## Quick recap

- An IPO is the process of a private company offering shares to the public for the first
  time.
- Retail investors apply using UPI, with the amount blocked, not debited, until allotment.
- Lot size sets the minimum shares per application; allotment can involve a lottery in
  oversubscribed issues.
- Listing risk means a share can list above or below its IPO price, in either direction.
- SME IPOs involve smaller companies, generally different norms, and often lower
  post-listing liquidity than mainboard IPOs.

## Check yourself

1. What happens to your blocked UPI amount if you are not allotted shares in an IPO?
   ::: details Show answer
   The block is released, and no amount is debited from your bank account.
   :::
2. Why might not every applicant receive an allotment in an IPO?
   ::: details Show answer
   If the IPO is oversubscribed, meaning applications exceed available shares, not all
   applicants receive shares; allotment for retail investors is often decided through a
   computerised lottery.
   :::
3. Does a share always list above its IPO price?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. Listing risk means a share can list above or below its IPO price, based on market
   demand on listing day; it is not guaranteed in either direction.
   :::
4. How does an SME IPO generally differ from a mainboard IPO?
   ::: details Show answer
   SME IPOs involve smaller companies, follow separate SME platform norms, and often have
   lower liquidity after listing compared to mainboard IPOs.
   :::
