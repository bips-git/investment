# How Trading Works

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- How buy and sell orders are actually matched on an exchange
- The difference between market, limit and stop-loss orders
- How settlement works, and delivery vs intraday trading
- What circuit limits are and why they exist
:::

## In plain words

Think of a crowded vegetable market where many buyers and sellers shout out prices at
once. A helper matches the closest matching buyer and seller together for each deal. A
stock exchange does the same thing electronically, matching buy and sell orders for
shares, at high speed and at large scale.

## How it works

### Order matching

When you place a buy or sell order through your broker (introduced in Unit 2.4), the
order reaches the exchange (NSE or BSE, from Unit 2.1), where it is matched against
opposite orders, based mainly on price and time priority: better prices are matched first,
and among equal prices, earlier orders are matched first.

### Types of orders

| Order type | What it does | When it typically executes |
|---|---|---|
| Market order | Buy or sell immediately at the best available current price | Almost immediately, price not guaranteed in advance |
| Limit order | Buy or sell only at a specified price or better | Only if the market reaches your specified price |
| Stop-loss order | Becomes a market or limit order once a specified trigger price is reached | Only after the trigger price is hit, to help limit losses |

**Market order** - an order to buy or sell immediately at the best price currently
available in the market. **Limit order** - an order to buy or sell only at a specific
price or better, which may not execute if the market never reaches that price. **Stop-loss
order** - an order that becomes active only after a specified trigger price is reached,
commonly used to limit losses on an existing position.

### Settlement

**Settlement** - the process of actually transferring shares to the buyer's demat account
and payment to the seller's bank account, after a trade is matched on the exchange. Indian
equity markets generally follow a T+1 settlement cycle, meaning settlement is completed
one business day after the trade date.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
Settlement cycle timelines are set by SEBI and exchanges, and have changed over recent
years. Confirm the current settlement cycle on the NSE or BSE website before relying on
exact timing.
:::

### Delivery vs intraday trading

**Delivery trading** - buying shares with the intention of holding them beyond the same
trading day, resulting in shares being credited to your demat account after settlement.
**Intraday trading** - buying and selling (or selling and buying) the same shares within
the same trading day, without taking delivery into a demat account.

| Feature | Delivery trading | Intraday trading |
|---|---|---|
| Holding period | Beyond the trading day | Within the same trading day |
| Demat account involvement | Shares credited to demat account | No delivery into demat account |
| Typical risk profile | Generally aligned with longer-term market risk, Unit 1.3 | Generally higher, due to short time frame and often leverage, covered in Chapter 6 |

### Circuit limits

**Circuit limit** (price band) - a maximum percentage a share's or index's price is
allowed to move, up or down, within a trading session, before trading is temporarily
paused or restricted, set by the exchange as a safeguard against extreme volatility.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
Circuit limit percentages vary by stock and can be revised by exchanges. Confirm the
current circuit limit for a specific stock on the NSE or BSE website before trading around
a volatile event.
:::

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Meena wants to buy a share currently trading around ₹250.

| Order type used | Instruction given | Illustrative outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Market order | Buy now, at best available price | Order executes almost immediately, around ₹250, exact price not guaranteed |
| Limit order | Buy only at ₹245 or lower | Order executes only if the price falls to ₹245 or below; otherwise it stays pending |
| Stop-loss order (on an existing holding) | Sell if price falls to ₹230 | Becomes active only if the price drops to ₹230, helping limit further loss |

This is illustrative, not a forecast. It shows how each order type gives different
control over price versus certainty of execution.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Using a market order in a fast-moving or illiquid stock, risking execution at an
  unexpectedly poor price.
- Setting a limit price far from the current market price, causing the order to remain
  unexecuted for a long time.
- Confusing intraday trading with delivery trading, and missing the requirement to square
  off intraday positions within the same day.
- Not accounting for circuit limits when planning to buy or sell during volatile market
  conditions.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Intraday trading and reliance on precise order timing carry meaningfully higher risk than
simple delivery-based investing. SEBI studies, referenced in Chapter 6, have found that
most individual intraday and derivative traders lose money over time. Beginners are
generally better served starting with simple delivery-based investing while learning.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
NISM workbooks describe exchange order matching as following price-time priority within an
electronic order book, and settlement as guaranteed by the clearing corporation, introduced
in Unit 2.1, which interposes itself between every buyer and seller to eliminate
counterparty risk. Circuit limits and settlement cycle rules are part of SEBI's broader
market stability and investor protection framework, designed to reduce the impact of
extreme, rapid price swings on ordinary investors.
:::

## Quick recap

- Orders are matched on the exchange mainly by price and time priority.
- Market orders prioritise speed of execution; limit orders prioritise price control;
  stop-loss orders trigger only after a specified price is reached.
- Settlement transfers shares and payment after a trade, generally on a T+1 cycle in India.
- Delivery trading holds shares beyond the trading day; intraday trading closes the
  position within the same day.
- Circuit limits restrict how far a price can move in a session, as a volatility
  safeguard.

## Check yourself

1. What is the main trade-off between a market order and a limit order?
   ::: details Show answer
   A market order prioritises immediate execution but does not guarantee the exact price. A
   limit order guarantees a maximum or minimum price but may not execute if the market never
   reaches that price.
   :::
2. What does T+1 settlement mean?
   ::: details Show answer
   It means settlement, the transfer of shares and payment, is completed one business day
   after the trade date.
   :::
3. What is the key difference between delivery trading and intraday trading?
   ::: details Show answer
   Delivery trading holds shares beyond the trading day, with shares credited to a demat
   account. Intraday trading buys and sells (or sells and buys) the same shares within the
   same trading day, without taking delivery.
   :::
4. What is the purpose of a circuit limit?
   ::: details Show answer
   It restricts how far a share's or index's price can move within a trading session,
   acting as a safeguard against extreme, rapid volatility.
   :::
