# What a Share Is

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- What owning a share actually means
- Why share prices move up and down
- What dividends, splits and bonus shares are
- How individual shares relate to market indices
:::

## In plain words

Think of a kirana shop owned jointly by ten friends, each holding an equal slice of
ownership. If the shop does well, each friend's slice becomes more valuable, and the shop
may share some profit with them. A **share** works the same way, but for companies that can
have thousands or millions of owners, and where ownership slices can be bought and sold
freely on an exchange.

## How it works

### What owning a share means

A **share** - a unit of ownership in a company. Owning shares of a company makes you a
part-owner, entitled to a proportional claim on its profits and assets, and usually the
right to vote on certain company matters.

### Why share prices move

A share's price is mainly determined by what buyers are willing to pay and what sellers
are willing to accept, at any given moment, on an exchange (introduced in Unit 2.1).
Prices move based on factors including:

1. The company's actual or expected profit and business performance.
2. Broader economic conditions, such as inflation or interest rates, introduced in
   Chapter 1.
3. Industry-specific news, such as regulatory changes or competition.
4. General investor sentiment and demand, which can move prices even without new company
   news.

### Market capitalisation, revisited

**Market capitalisation** (market cap), introduced in Unit 3.2, is calculated as:

```
market cap = share price x total number of shares outstanding
```

A company's market cap changes constantly, since share price changes constantly, even if
the number of shares outstanding stays the same.

### Dividends

A **dividend** - a portion of a company's profit distributed to shareholders, usually as
cash, per share held. Not all companies pay dividends; some reinvest profits back into the
business instead.

### Stock splits

A **stock split** - dividing each existing share into multiple new shares, reducing the
price per share proportionally, without changing the total value of an investor's
holding.

| Before split | After a 1:2 split (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| 100 shares at ₹500 each | 200 shares at ₹250 each |
| Total value: ₹50,000 | Total value: ₹50,000 |

This is illustrative, not a forecast. A split does not, by itself, change the underlying
value of the company or an investor's total holding.

### Bonus shares

A **bonus share** - an additional share issued free to existing shareholders, in a fixed
ratio to shares already held, funded from the company's reserves, rather than by
shareholders paying extra money.

| Before bonus | After a 1:1 bonus (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| 100 shares held | 200 shares held |
| Approximate share price before | Approximate share price roughly halves, all else being equal |

This is illustrative, not a forecast. Like a split, a bonus issue does not, by itself,
change the underlying value of a shareholder's total holding.

### Indices, revisited

An **index**, introduced in Unit 3.4, such as the Nifty 50 or Sensex, tracks a defined
group of shares to represent overall market or sector performance. Individual share prices
can move differently from the broader index, even on the same day.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Company X has 10 crore shares outstanding, and its share price is ₹200.

| Item | Value (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Share price | ₹200 |
| Total shares outstanding | 10 crore |
| Market cap | ₹2,000 crore |

10 crore means 10,00,00,000, and ₹2,000 crore means ₹2,000,00,00,000, using the Indian
numbering system introduced in Unit 1.1. If the company declares a dividend of ₹5 per
share, a shareholder with 100 shares would receive ₹500, before any applicable tax,
covered in Chapter 8. This is illustrative, not a forecast.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Assuming a lower share price alone means a company is "cheaper" or a better value,
  without comparing to fundamentals covered in Unit 5.4.
- Confusing a stock split or bonus issue with the company actually creating new value.
- Believing dividends are guaranteed or fixed, like interest on an FD, introduced in Unit
  1.4.
- Assuming all shares in an index move identically with that index every day.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Share prices can be volatile, as introduced in Unit 1.3. A company's share price falling
sharply on bad news, or rising sharply on rumours, is a normal market occurrence, not
necessarily a sign of fraud, though sudden extreme moves are also sometimes linked to
manipulation, discussed further in Chapter 9.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Bodie, Kane and Marcus's *Investments* frames share ownership as a residual claim on a
company's assets and earnings, meaning shareholders are paid after debt holders and other
obligations are met, which is why equity, introduced in Unit 1.4, generally carries higher
risk than debt instruments. Market efficiency theories, referenced in Malkiel's *A Random
Walk Down Wall Street* and discussed in Unit 3.4, argue that share prices tend to quickly
reflect publicly available information, though the degree of this efficiency is debated
among academics and practitioners.
:::

## Quick recap

- A share represents a unit of ownership in a company, with a proportional claim on
  profits and assets.
- Share prices move based on company performance, economic conditions, industry news and
  investor sentiment.
- Dividends distribute profit to shareholders; they are not guaranteed like fixed deposit
  interest.
- Stock splits and bonus shares change the number of shares held, without changing total
  underlying value by themselves.
- Indices track groups of shares, but individual shares can move differently from the
  broader index.

## Check yourself

1. What does owning a share of a company actually entitle you to?
   ::: details Show answer
   A proportional claim on the company's profits and assets, and usually the right to vote
   on certain company matters.
   :::
2. Does a stock split change the total value of your holding?
   ::: details Show answer
   No, not by itself. A split increases the number of shares you hold while
   proportionally reducing the price per share, keeping total value the same, all else
   being equal.
   :::
3. Are dividends guaranteed, like interest on a fixed deposit?
   ::: details Show answer
   No. Dividends depend on a company's profit and decision to distribute it, and are not
   fixed or guaranteed the way FD interest is.
   :::
4. Can an individual share's price move differently from a broader market index on the
   same day?
   ::: details Show answer
   Yes. An index tracks a defined group of shares, but any single share can move up or down
   differently from that overall index on a given day.
   :::
