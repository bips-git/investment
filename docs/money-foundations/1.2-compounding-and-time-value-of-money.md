# Compounding and Time Value of Money

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- The difference between simple interest and compound interest
- A quick shortcut to estimate doubling time: the rule of 72
- What present value and future value mean
- Why starting to invest early matters more than investing a large amount later
:::

## In plain words

Imagine a kirana shop owner who reinvests every rupee of profit back into more stock every
month, instead of spending it. Over time, the shop grows faster and faster, because profit
is earned not only on the original stock but also on the profit already added back.
This snowball effect, where earnings themselves start earning more, is called
**compounding**.

## How it works

### Simple interest vs compound interest

**Simple interest** is interest calculated only on the original amount, every period.
**Compound interest** is interest calculated on the original amount plus all interest
already earned.

| Year | Simple interest on ₹1,00,000 at 10% | Compound interest on ₹1,00,000 at 10% |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹1,10,000 | ₹1,10,000 |
| 2 | ₹1,20,000 | ₹1,21,000 |
| 3 | ₹1,30,000 | ₹1,33,100 |
| 5 | ₹1,50,000 | ₹1,61,051 |
| 10 | ₹2,00,000 | ₹2,59,374 |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. The gap between the two columns grows wider
every year, because compound interest keeps earning on its own earnings.

### The rule of 72

The **rule of 72** is a quick shortcut to estimate how many years it takes for money to
double at a given compound annual rate.

```
years to double ≈ 72 / annual rate (in percent)
```

| Annual rate | Approximate years to double |
|---|---|
| 6% | 12 years |
| 9% | 8 years |
| 12% | 6 years |

This is illustrative, not a forecast, and is only an approximation. It is most accurate
for rates between about 6% and 12%.

### Present value and future value

**Future value (FV)** is what a sum of money today will grow to, after a given number of
years, at a given rate of return. **Present value (PV)** is the reverse: how much a future
sum of money is worth today, given a rate of return.

```
FV = PV x (1 + rate) ^ number of years
PV = FV / (1 + rate) ^ number of years
```

1. Decide the amount, the rate of return, and the number of years.
2. To find future value, grow today's amount forward using the formula above.
3. To find present value, discount a future amount backward using the same formula.

### Why starting early wins

The number of years money stays invested matters as much as the rate of return, because
compounding needs time to build up.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Two friends, Aman and Bina, both plan to invest until age 60. Both expect a 10% annual
return, illustrative and not a forecast.

- Aman invests ₹1,00,000 once, starting at age 25 (35 years to grow).
- Bina invests ₹1,00,000 once, starting at age 35 (25 years to grow).

| Investor | Start age | Years invested | Value at age 60 (illustrative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aman | 25 | 35 | About ₹28,10,000 |
| Bina | 35 | 25 | About ₹10,83,500 |

Bina invested the exact same amount as Aman, but ten years later. Her final amount is less
than half of Aman's. The ten extra years of compounding made the biggest difference, not
the amount invested.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Waiting to "save a bigger amount" before starting, and losing years of compounding.
- Confusing simple interest quotes with compound interest, especially in loan or scheme
  brochures.
- Using the rule of 72 for very high or very low rates, where it is less accurate.
- Ignoring the effect of inflation and tax on the real, after-tax return, as covered in
  Unit 1.1.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Some schemes advertise a high "return" without stating clearly whether it is simple or
compound, or over how many years. Always ask for the exact compounding method, the exact
rate, and whether the rate is before or after tax.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
The time value of money is a foundational concept in corporate finance, covered in detail
in Brealey and Myers's *Principles of Corporate Finance*. The general future value formula
for periodic compounding is:

```
FV = PV x (1 + r/n) ^ (n x t)
```

where `r` is the annual rate, `n` is the number of compounding periods per year, and `t` is
the number of years. When `n` equals 1, this simplifies to the annual compounding formula
shown above. The same logic underlies annuity formulas used to value regular investments
such as a **SIP** (Systematic Investment Plan), covered in Chapter 4.
:::

## Quick recap

- Simple interest is earned only on the original amount; compound interest is earned on
  the original amount plus all interest already added.
- The rule of 72 gives a quick estimate of doubling time: 72 divided by the annual rate.
- Future value grows a sum forward in time; present value discounts a sum back to today.
- Time invested often matters more than the amount invested, because compounding needs
  years to build momentum.
- Starting early, even with a small amount, usually beats starting late with a larger
  amount.

   ::: details Show answer
   Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount every period. Compound interest
   is calculated on the original amount plus all interest already earned, so it grows faster

   Present value. It tells you how much a future amount is worth today, given an expected
   rate of return.
