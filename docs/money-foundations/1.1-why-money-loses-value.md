# Why Money Loses Value

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- Why the same rupee buys less every year
- What **CPI** is and how India measures inflation
- The difference between **nominal return** and **real return**
- Why "safe" savings can still lose value over time
:::

## In plain words

Think of a chai stall. Ten years ago, one cup of chai cost ₹5. Today it costs ₹15 or more.
The chai has not changed. Your money has become weaker. This slow weakening of money's power
to buy things is called **inflation** - a general and sustained rise in prices over time, which
means each rupee buys fewer goods and services than before.

## How it works

### What is purchasing power

**Purchasing power** is the amount of goods or services one rupee can actually buy.
When prices rise, purchasing power falls, even though the number of rupees in your pocket
stays the same.

1. You have ₹100 today.
2. Prices rise by 6 percent this year.
3. Next year, you need ₹106 to buy the same basket of goods you bought for ₹100 today.
4. If you still have only ₹100, you can now buy less than before.

### How India measures inflation: CPI

**CPI** (Consumer Price Index) - a number published every month that tracks the average
price of a fixed basket of goods and services (food, fuel, housing, clothing, and more)
that a typical household buys. It is released by the National Statistical Office and used
by the Reserve Bank of India (**RBI**) to judge inflation.

The inflation rate is the percentage change in CPI over a period, usually one year.

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
CPI numbers and the current inflation rate change every month. Confirm the latest figure
on the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) website or the RBI website
before using it anywhere important.
:::

### Nominal return vs real return

When your bank fixed deposit (FD) pays you interest, it quotes a **nominal return** - the
percentage return on your money before adjusting for inflation. But what matters for your
actual buying power is the **real return** - the return left after subtracting inflation from
the nominal return.

A simple approximate formula:

```
real return ≈ nominal return - inflation rate
```

| Item | Value (illustrative) |
|---|---|
| Nominal return on FD | 6.5% per year |
| Inflation rate (CPI) | 5.0% per year |
| Approximate real return | 1.5% per year |

This table is illustrative, not a forecast. Actual rates change every year.

### Comparing outcomes over time

| Scenario | Money today | Inflation | Value after 1 year (real terms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash under the mattress | ₹1,00,000 | 6% | Worth about ₹94,340 in today's prices |
| FD at 6.5% nominal | ₹1,00,000 | 6% | Grows to ₹1,06,500, worth about ₹1,00,472 in today's prices |

₹1,00,000 is one lakh rupees. In the Indian number system, 1 lakh = 1,00,000 and
1 crore = 1,00,00,000 (ten million). Numbers are grouped as thousand, lakh, crore instead
of thousand, million, billion.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Radha keeps ₹50,000 in cash at home for two years instead of a bank account.

| Year | Cash held | Inflation that year | What ₹50,000 can now buy, in today's prices |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start | ₹50,000 | - | ₹50,000 worth of goods |
| After year 1 | ₹50,000 | 6% | About ₹47,170 worth of goods |
| After year 2 | ₹50,000 | 6% | About ₹44,500 worth of goods |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast. Radha's rupee count never changed, but what
those rupees can buy kept shrinking. This is why simply holding cash is not "safe" from
inflation's point of view.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Looking only at the nominal return and ignoring inflation.
- Assuming cash or a locker is "safe" because the rupee amount never falls.
- Comparing returns from different years without checking what inflation was in each year.
- Forgetting that essentials like school fees, medicines and rent often rise faster than
  the average CPI number.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Some sellers of investment products quote only the nominal return to make an option look
attractive. Always ask: "What is the real return, after inflation and after tax?" A return
that looks big before inflation can be very small, or even negative, after it.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
The Fisher equation, described in standard investment textbooks such as Bodie, Kane and
Marcus's *Investments*, gives the precise relationship between nominal and real rates:

```
(1 + nominal rate) = (1 + real rate) x (1 + inflation rate)
```

The simple subtraction method used above is an approximation that works well when
inflation and returns are both small. At higher inflation levels, the gap between the
approximation and the exact Fisher equation grows larger, so the exact formula is
preferred in formal analysis.
:::

## Quick recap

- Inflation is a sustained rise in prices, which reduces purchasing power.
- CPI is the official measure used to track inflation in India.
- Nominal return is the return before inflation; real return is what is left after inflation.
- Holding only cash, or an FD with a low real return, can still mean losing value over time.
- Always ask about the real, after-inflation, after-tax return before judging any option.

## Check yourself

1. What does a rising CPI number tell you about prices in the economy?
   ::: details Show answer
   A rising CPI means the average price of a fixed basket of goods and services is going up,
   which signals that inflation is happening.
   :::
2. If a fixed deposit pays 7% nominal interest and inflation is 5%, what is the approximate
   real return?
   ::: details Show answer
   About 2% (7% minus 5%), using the simple approximation method.
   :::
3. Why can cash kept at home lose value even though the rupee amount does not change?
   ::: details Show answer
   Because rising prices mean that same rupee amount buys fewer goods and services over time.
   This is the effect of inflation reducing purchasing power.
   :::
4. Which Indian body publishes the CPI, and which uses it to guide interest rate decisions?
   ::: details Show answer
   The National Statistical Office (under MoSPI) publishes CPI. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI)
   uses it to guide monetary policy and interest rate decisions.
   :::
