# Risk and Return

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="8 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- The main types of risk an investment can carry
- What volatility means and why it matters
- Why higher expected return usually means higher risk
- The difference between risk appetite and risk capacity
:::

## In plain words

Think of two ways to travel to your school or office: walking, which is slow but very
predictable, and riding a two-wheeler in heavy traffic, which is faster but less certain
and carries more chance of a bad surprise. Investing works in a similar way. Options that
promise higher returns usually carry a higher chance that things do not go as planned.
This chance of an unwanted outcome is called **risk**.

## How it works

### Main types of investment risk

| Risk type | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Market risk | The value of an investment falls because market prices move | A stock's price falls sharply when the broader market falls |
| Inflation risk | Returns fail to keep pace with rising prices, covered in Unit 1.1 | A fixed deposit's real return turns negative in a high-inflation year |
| Credit risk | A borrower fails to repay interest or principal on time | A company defaults on the bonds it had issued |
| Liquidity risk | An investment cannot be sold quickly at a fair price when cash is needed | Real estate can take months to sell at the expected price |

### Volatility

**Volatility** is how much and how often the price of an investment moves up and down
over time. An investment with high volatility can gain or lose a large percentage of its
value in a short period. An investment with low volatility tends to move in small, steady
steps.

1. Track the price of an investment over many days or months.
2. Measure how far the price typically swings above or below its average.
3. A larger typical swing means higher volatility, and usually higher risk.

### The risk-return trade-off

The **risk-return trade-off** is the general principle that investments offering a higher
expected return usually carry higher risk, and safer investments usually offer lower
expected return.

| Investment type | Typical risk | Typical expected return |
|---|---|---|
| Bank savings account | Very low | Very low |
| Government bond | Low | Low to moderate |
| Corporate bond | Moderate | Moderate |
| Equity (shares) | High | Potentially high, not guaranteed |

This table shows typical patterns, illustrative and not a forecast. Actual outcomes for
any single investment can differ from these general patterns.

### Risk appetite vs risk capacity

**Risk appetite** is how much risk an investor is willing to take, based on their comfort
and mindset. **Risk capacity** is how much risk an investor can actually afford to take,
based on their finances, age, goals and responsibilities.

A person can have a high risk appetite (comfortable with big swings) but low risk capacity
(no emergency fund, school fees due next month). In that case, the safer path is to follow
risk capacity, not appetite.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Consider two investors, each putting ₹1,00,000 into a high-volatility option for one year.

| Investor | Emergency fund | School fees due soon | Suitable risk level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deepak | 6 months of expenses saved | No | Can reasonably take higher risk |
| Farha | No emergency fund | Yes, in 3 months | Should avoid high risk for this money |

If the investment falls by 20% in that year, illustrative and not a forecast, Deepak can
wait for a recovery because his other needs are covered. Farha may be forced to sell during
a downturn to pay urgent expenses, locking in a loss she could have avoided.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Chasing the highest advertised return without checking what risk it carries.
- Ignoring liquidity risk, and investing money that will be needed soon in something hard
  to sell quickly.
- Assuming risk appetite alone decides how much risk to take, while ignoring risk capacity.
- Treating "government-backed" as meaning zero risk, when interest rate and inflation risk
  can still apply.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
Be very cautious of anyone who promises high returns with "no risk". Every investment
carries some risk. A claim of guaranteed high returns with zero risk is a common warning
sign of a scam, covered in detail in Chapter 9.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
Standard portfolio theory, as presented in Bodie, Kane and Marcus's *Investments* and in
Prasanna Chandra's *Investment Analysis and Portfolio Management*, measures volatility
using **standard deviation** of historical returns, a statistical measure of how much
returns typically deviate from their average. Total risk is often separated into
**systematic risk** (market-wide risk that cannot be removed by diversification) and
**unsystematic risk** (risk specific to one company or sector, which diversification can
reduce). Diversification is covered further in Unit 7.3.
:::

## Quick recap

- Key risk types include market, inflation, credit and liquidity risk.
- Volatility measures how much an investment's price swings over time.
- The risk-return trade-off means higher expected return usually comes with higher risk.
- Risk appetite is willingness to take risk; risk capacity is the ability to afford it.
- When appetite and capacity conflict, following capacity is usually the safer choice.

## Check yourself

1. What is the difference between market risk and liquidity risk?
   ::: details Show answer
   Market risk is the chance that an investment's value falls because prices move.
   Liquidity risk is the chance that an investment cannot be sold quickly at a fair price
   when cash is needed, even if its stated value has not fallen.
   :::
2. Why might an investment with high volatility not suit someone with school fees due in
   three months?
   ::: details Show answer
   High volatility means the investment's value could be much lower exactly when the money
   is needed, forcing a sale at a loss. Money needed soon usually needs low risk and high
   liquidity.
   :::
3. What is the difference between risk appetite and risk capacity?
   ::: details Show answer
   Risk appetite is how much risk an investor is comfortable taking. Risk capacity is how
   much risk an investor can actually afford, based on finances and goals.
   :::
4. According to the risk-return trade-off, what would you generally expect if an option
   promises unusually high returns with almost no risk?
   ::: details Show answer
   This is unusual and worth investigating carefully. Genuinely low-risk options generally
   do not offer very high returns; a promise of both often signals a scam.
   :::
