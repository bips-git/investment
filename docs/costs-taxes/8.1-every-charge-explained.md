# Every Charge Explained

<Badge type="tip" text="Beginner" /> <Badge type="info" text="9 min read" />

::: info 🎯 What you will learn
- The main charges applied on a stock market transaction
- What a contract note is, and how to read it
- Why small charges on every trade can add up over time
- Where to check the exact, current rate for each charge
:::

## In plain words

Think of buying a train ticket: the fare itself is only part of the final price. There is
often a reservation fee, a service charge, and sometimes a small tax added on top. Buying
or selling shares works similarly: the price you see is not the only amount involved;
several smaller charges apply on top of it.

## How it works

### The main charges on a share transaction

| Charge | What it is |
|---|---|
| Brokerage | The fee charged by your broker (introduced in Unit 2.4) for executing a trade |
| DP charges | A fee charged by the Depository Participant, usually per scrip, when shares are sold from a demat account (introduced in Unit 2.3) |
| STT (Securities Transaction Tax) | A tax levied by the government on the value of securities transactions |
| Exchange transaction charges | Fees charged by the exchange (NSE or BSE, Unit 2.1) for facilitating the trade |
| GST (Goods and Services Tax) | A tax applied on brokerage and certain other charges |
| Stamp duty | A state-level duty charged on the transfer of securities |

::: warning 📅 Check the latest
Exact rates for STT, exchange transaction charges, GST, stamp duty, DP charges, and
brokerage change over time and can vary by broker, transaction type (delivery vs
intraday), and instrument. [VERIFY: check your broker's current tariff sheet, and SEBI or
exchange circulars, for the exact current rates applicable to your specific transaction
type before relying on precise figures.]
:::

### What a contract note is

A **contract note** - a legally mandated document, issued by your broker after each
trading day in which you transacted, itemising every trade executed, the price, and every
individual charge applied, along with the net amount payable or receivable.

1. Check the trade details: security name, quantity, and price for each transaction.
2. Check each individual charge line: brokerage, STT, exchange charges, GST, stamp duty,
   and any other applicable fee.
3. Check the final net settlement amount, which reflects the trade value adjusted for all
   charges.
4. Compare this against your bank statement or trading account ledger, to confirm accuracy.

### Why small charges add up

Each individual charge on a single trade may look small, but frequent trading multiplies
this effect across many transactions, similar in principle to how a small expense ratio
difference compounds over time, discussed in Unit 3.3.

| Trading pattern | Illustrative effect of charges |
|---|---|
| A few trades per year | Charges are a small, generally manageable part of overall cost |
| Frequent daily or intraday trading | Charges accumulate quickly across many transactions, meaningfully reducing net returns |

This is illustrative, not a forecast. The exact impact depends on trade frequency, size,
and the specific charges applicable to each transaction type.

::: tip 💡 Real-life example
Vivek buys shares worth ₹50,000 in a delivery trade, illustrative and not a forecast.

| Charge | Illustrative amount |
|---|---|
| Brokerage | ₹20 |
| STT | ₹50 |
| Exchange transaction charges | ₹15 |
| GST (on brokerage and exchange charges) | ₹6 |
| Stamp duty | ₹15 |
| DP charges (applicable only when selling) | Not applicable at purchase |
| Approximate total charges | ₹106 |

All figures are illustrative, not a forecast, and actual rates vary by broker and current
regulation. This shows the general structure of charges rather than a specific quote.
:::

::: warning ⚠️ Common mistakes
- Assuming brokerage is the only charge on a trade, ignoring STT, exchange charges, GST
  and stamp duty.
- Not checking DP charges when selling shares, since they can apply per scrip, regardless
  of the number of shares sold.
- Ignoring the cumulative effect of charges on frequent trading.
- Not reviewing contract notes regularly to confirm charges match what was expected.
:::

::: danger 🚨 Safety check
If a contract note shows charges you do not recognise, or a net settlement amount that
does not match your expectation, raise a query with your broker promptly. Keeping and
reviewing contract notes also helps if a dispute arises later.
:::

::: details 🧠 Go deeper (university level)
SEBI mandates that brokers issue a contract note for every trading day on which a client
transacts, specifying the exact format and required disclosures, referenced in NISM
workbooks. STT is levied under a separate tax statute administered by the government,
distinct from SEBI's regulatory charges, while stamp duty on securities transactions is
governed by a centralised collection mechanism agreed between states, following
amendments to the Indian Stamp Act.
:::

## Quick recap

- A share transaction involves several charges: brokerage, DP charges, STT, exchange
  charges, GST, and stamp duty.
- A contract note itemises every trade and charge for a trading day, with the net
  settlement amount.
- Charges that look small individually can add up meaningfully with frequent trading.
- Always confirm exact, current charge rates with your broker or official sources, since
  they change periodically.
